# Win ten seconds, then prove you're real

Visitors "dive in" when a page passes three tests in order. First it has to look simple, familiar and well made in under a second. Then it has to say plainly what it offers and for whom within about ten seconds. Then, every time the visitor scrolls, it has to give them more reason to believe a real, reachable, competent person stands behind it. For a one-person studio selling redesigns to Italian renovation firms, B&Bs, agriturismi and wedding venues, the evidence points to a clear priority order. The weightiest levers are a plain-Italian, offer-first hero built around a real before/after of a sector website; honest substitutes for the client proof the studio does not yet have (a named founder with a face, the Partita IVA, published "from €" prices, a written process, a narrow risk-reversal offer); and a phone-first build that loads fast and never takes over the scroll. Colour, typography and motion matter mostly because they shape that first gestalt judgment and because, for a design studio, the site is the product sample. A layout bug or a janky scroll on a mid-range Android reads directly as "this is the work I'd get." Hue choice, button colour and award-style 3D effects have weak or no evidence of lifting enquiries from non-designer owners, and heavy motion carries measurable speed and accessibility costs for an audience that is mostly on phones and often over 40. The report closes with a weighted 100-point checklist. It is a synthesis built from the published weights of MECLABS, Awwwards, Lighthouse and HubSpot, the Stanford credibility research and the specific evidence below. It is not a validated instrument.

## Simple, familiar pages win the first 50 milliseconds

People decide whether a site is visually appealing almost instantly, and later judgments stay anchored to that first read. Lindgaard and colleagues showed homepages for 50 ms and found **appeal ratings at 50 ms correlated highly with ratings at 500 ms**. They concluded that "visual appeal can be assessed within 50 ms" ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Attention-web-designers:-You-have-50-milliseconds-a-Lindgaard-Fernandes/f9715b117c57d4e7064afe1c1cb95d5bf4cc1831)). A 2011 follow-up found that **trustworthiness and perceived usability judged at 50 ms are "largely driven by visual appeal"** ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1959022.1959023)). So the first impression is also the first trust judgment.

What produces a good first impression is now well established. Tuch and colleagues at Google and the University of Basel varied 119 real sites on visual complexity and prototypicality, meaning how much a site looks like a typical member of its category. **Low complexity plus high prototypicality was rated most appealing, and complexity registered even at 17 ms**, before prototypicality did ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/)). Google's own summary was "users love simple and familiar designs" ([Google Research Blog](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)). Reinecke's CHI 2013 models found that "colorfulness plays a role, but is not nearly as important as the overall visual complexity of a site" ([Harvard](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2013/reinecke13predicting.shtml)). Complexity also has a functional cost: in a 165-person study, **more complex pages slowed search and reduced recall** ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3313831.3376849?cid=99659527940)). Preferences vary with demographics. A popular-science summary of Reinecke and Gajos's 2014 work reports that people over 40 and women preferred more colourful sites ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/sg/blog/brain-wise/201510/age-gender-geography-and-visual-appeal-0)). For an older Italian audience, warm colour is tolerable and possibly welcome. Clutter is not. No Italy-specific preference data surfaced.

For this studio, prototypicality is the counter-intuitive lesson. A site selling design is tempted to be unconventional. But visitors judge appeal fastest when the page looks like what they expect a studio site to look like: a navigation bar, a headline that states the offer, visible work and a contact route. Full-screen "slide" scenes without that skeleton work against the effect Tuch measured. The Stanford Web Credibility study shows how much is riding on this. Among 2,684 people evaluating live sites, **"design look" was the most frequently mentioned credibility factor, in 46.1% of comments**, ahead of information structure at about 28.5% ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/997078.997097); [Consumer Reports](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility)). That figure is a share of comments, not of people. The aesthetic-usability effect cuts both ways: attractive interfaces are perceived as easier to use and earn forgiveness for minor flaws, but **aesthetics cannot offset severe usability failures** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/)). For a studio, a beautiful hero that breaks on a phone does more damage than a plain one that works.

Benchmark studios show two ways to resolve the tension between craft and clarity. Award-gallery studios such as Kota and Collins open with a statement headline or showreel and put proof directly under the hero ([NanoGlobals](https://nanoglobals.com/marketing-agency-websites/); [Teamwork](https://www.teamwork.com/blog/marketing-agency-websites/)). Productized small-business studios open with the offer itself. Neon Studios' title is "Small Business Web Design — $5,000, Live in 14 Days" ([theneonstudios.com](https://www.theneonstudios.com/)), and SiteReadyWeb leads with a price and "pay only if you approve" ([sitereadyweb.com](https://sitereadyweb.com/)). A studio selling to local owners needs the second structure, dressed with the polish of the first. Personality should come from voice and one signature interaction, not from hiding the offer.

## Ten seconds and two screens decide whether anyone scrolls

Once the gestalt is judged, the visitor reads for a reason to stay. Nielsen's analysis of Microsoft Research dwell-time data found that **leaving is most likely in the first seconds and becomes less likely the longer a visitor stays**, so "to gain several minutes of user attention, you must clearly communicate your value proposition within 10 seconds" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)). Attention is also concentrated. NN/g's 2018 eyetracking (120 participants, 130,000+ fixations) found **57% of viewing time above the fold and 74% within the first two screenfuls**, with a sharp drop after the fold that has not changed since 2010 ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). **80% of fixations fell on the left half of the screen** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/horizontal-attention-leans-left/)). These are desktop figures. On a phone the first screen holds far less, so the priorities are stricter.

From this, NN/g draws a firm rule: "users do scroll, but only if what's above the fold is promising enough," and "designs shouldn't need an arrow to tell users to scroll" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto/)). The hero must carry a descriptive tagline and value proposition rather than a generic welcome, because purely decorative graphics get skipped ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)). The first viewport of this studio's site, on a 375 px phone and on desktop, should therefore hold four things. The first is a sentence a renovation-firm owner understands on one read (for example, siti rifatti per imprese edili, B&B e location per matrimoni). The second is a reason to believe it, ideally a real before/after of a sector site. The third is one primary action. The fourth is a visible sliver of the next section. Rotating heroes undermine this, because many people see only the first frame of a carousel ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)).

Minimalist layouts carry a specific, documented failure: the **"illusion of completeness" or false floor**. A full-viewport hero, a full-width rule or a wide band of empty space can make the page look finished, and visitors stop. NN/g notes that false floors "are increasingly common with modern minimalist designs." The fix is to let content visibly peek above the fold ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/illusion-of-completeness/)). This is where whitespace turns into dead space. Whitespace is a grouping tool, with tight gaps binding a heading to its content and wider gaps separating groups ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/)). It becomes harmful when a whole screen carries no content cue, when it separates a label from what it labels, or when it makes a section look like an ending. The popular "20–30% whitespace" rule has no research behind it ([DocHipo](https://www.dochipo.com/white-space-in-graphic-design/)). The practical rule is that every viewport-height slice of the page shows at least one meaningful heading, image or element. A hero at roughly 80–90% of viewport height, so the next section shows, is practitioner advice rather than tested ([Developers Journey](https://developersjourney.substack.com/p/above-the-fold-in-web-design-and-development)), but it follows directly from the false-floor finding.

Further down the page, headings carry the argument. The F-pattern is not a law. It is what readers fall into when text lacks formatting ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). The efficient alternative is **layer-cake scanning, where readers fixate mostly on headings and subheadings and dip selectively into the body**. It mostly lets people find what they came for ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)). A heading like "Il nostro metodo" wastes this. "In 3 settimane il tuo sito nuovo, senza che tu scriva una riga" delivers the message to someone who reads nothing else. The Z-pattern and Gutenberg diagram can still organise a sparse hero, but they have little empirical support on web pages ([Vanseo Design](https://vanseodesign.com/web-design/3-design-layouts/)). The two-screen attention cliff means every later section must re-earn the scroll with a payoff, the "information foraging" logic NN/g describes ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/scrolling-information-foraging/)).

## Colour and type signal craft but rarely move enquiries

The colour research that holds up concerns contrast, restraint and salience, not hue magic. Labrecque and Milne's four studies link red to perceived excitement and blue to competence, and show that **saturation amplifies arousal and excitement** ([JAMS abstract](https://colab.ws/articles/10.1007%2Fs11747-010-0245-y)). Older advertising research found that higher chroma raised arousal and lighter values raised relaxation ([IIMB](https://repository.iimb.ac.in/handle/123456789/521)). No primary research isolates orange, and no peer-reviewed study of Italian web-colour meanings turned up. "Best button colour" tests measure **contrast with the surrounding page, not the hue** ([EyeQuant](https://www.eyequant.com/blog/button-color-on-websites)). This is the von Restorff isolation effect: the item that differs from its surroundings gets noticed ([Switas](https://www.switas.com/glossary/von-restorff-effect)). A warm neutral palette with one saturated accent fits the evidence, provided the accent is reserved for action. If the same orange fills sections, badges and illustrations, the CTA stops being the isolated element. A lot of saturated orange can also read as "promotion" energy to cautious owners rather than calm competence. That is an inference from the arousal findings, not a tested result.

Contrast is where colour choices most often fail. WCAG requires **4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text, and 3:1 for UI component boundaries** ([W3C 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html); [W3C 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). The studio's own tokens show how easily a brand accent slips. Measured with the WCAG formula, mandarino #ff5b22 on the cream background #fff8f2 is **2.95:1**, which fails for any text and for thin outlines. White text on that orange is 3.1:1, so it passes only for large text. Dark inchiostro #24130c on the orange is 5.77:1 and passes, which is how the token file intends it to be used. A darker orange such as #c2410c reaches about 5.2:1 with white text. Secondary text at 6.8:1 passes comfortably and should not be lightened for "elegance."

Typography follows the same logic. Eyetracking shows **light and ultralight weights are less legible than regular and bold, and raise cognitive load** ([Burmistrov et al., NordiCHI 2016](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2971485.2996745)). The penalty applies mainly at text sizes, because strokes thicken at display sizes ([Penn State](https://accessibility.psu.edu/2021/10/beware-light-weight-fonts)). A light display weight at 40 px and above is defensible. The same weight in captions, buttons or a 32 px mobile heading is not. Body text should run about **50–75 characters per line** on desktop ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/line-length-readability)) and closer to 40–60 on mobile ([EC](https://ec.europa.eu/component-library/ec/guidelines/typography)). Google's only hard mobile threshold is that text under 12 px is hard to read ([web.dev](https://web.dev/font-size)). The common 16 px body minimum is a convention, and it also prevents iOS input zoom. No research covers extended or wide display faces. The visible cost is layout: at very large sizes on a 375 px screen, wide type fits two or three words per line, splits long Italian words like "ristrutturazioni", and pushes the CTA below the fold.

A newer concern is the template or AI-default look, with its purple gradients, Inter and three-icon card grids. Practitioners describe it as instantly recognisable ([Noqta](https://noqta.tn/en/blog/ai-design-slop-overused-ui-patterns-fix-2026)), but **no peer-reviewed study shows that visitors detect it or trust it less**. Widely shared statistics such as "82% of AI sites share layouts" are unsourced ([DEV](https://dev.to/steve_oak/vibe-coding-wont-make-your-product-stand-out-heres-what-will-4bj)). The defensible argument goes through memorability and the studio's own claim. Sameness makes a studio hard to tell apart from competitors, and following category norms still aids fluency ([CBS thesis](https://research.cbs.dk/en/studentProjects/competent-blue-and-exciting-red-examining-the-effect-of-logo-colo/)). Distinctiveness should therefore come from content, meaning real people, real work and local specificity, rather than from breaking conventions in navigation or CTA placement.

## Real faces and real work hold the eye; decoration does not

Imagery has the clearest attention evidence of any visual lever. NN/g's eyetracking found that **big, feel-good decorative images are ignored, while photos of real people and real products are studied as content**. On one team page, visitors spent 10% more time on real staff portraits than on bios that took up 316% more space ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)). This comes from a single 2010 test page, but it is widely corroborated. NN/g's About-Us research adds that realistic photography makes organisations seem authentic and transparent ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-information-on-websites/)). In a BrightLocal consumer panel, **64% trusted a real business-owner photo more than clip-art or stock**. The sample is small, so treat this as directional ([BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/images-inspire-trust-local-business-website/)). For a one-person studio, a named, locally recognisable founder photo is the best-supported image on the site.

Illustrations are not penalised, but they forfeit attention. In a 492-person study, a photo, an AI illustration and a cartoon of the same farmer showed **no significant credibility difference, yet 410 respondents preferred the photo against 43 and 39** ([New Prairie Press](https://journals.newprairiepress.org/jac/article/id/20785/)). Gaze cues can steer the eye. A face looking toward the product drew more attention to it and improved message memory compared with direct eye contact, which pulls attention to the face itself ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3941030)). A founder photo near the headline works better looking toward the CTA, while an About section benefits from direct gaze. The literature here is thin.

The studio's strongest image is a **real screenshot or before/after of a sector website**. In NN/g's terms it is "product" imagery, so it gets looked at, and the owner immediately recognises "a site for someone like me." A device mockup counts as content only when it frames identifiable real or honestly labelled work. No research shows that 3D phone frames add trust in their own right. AI imagery is a risk to manage, not a ban. NN/g found **no trust penalty for undisclosed AI hero images** on a consulting page ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/ai-generated-images)), but disclosed AI images reduced trust in a 1,171-person news study ([SSRC MediaWell](https://mediawell.ssrc.org/citations/perceived-legitimacy-matters-building-public-trust-and-acceptance-of-ai-generated-news-images-through-strategic-ai-disclosure/)). For a studio whose pitch is authenticity, AI-generated "team" photos or invented businesses presented as real are a trust failure waiting to be found.

## With no clients yet, transparency has to stand in for testimonials

The credibility frameworks agree on what to show when there is no reputation to borrow. NN/g's trustworthiness model, retested in 2016, names **design quality, up-front disclosure, comprehensive and current content, and connection to the rest of the web** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/)). Stanford's guidelines tell sites to show there is a real organisation behind them, to show trustworthy people, to make contact easy, to make information verifiable, and to avoid errors and typos ([Stanford](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines)). For an Italian studio part of this is mandatory. **Art. 35 DPR 633/1972 requires the Partita IVA on the homepage of a business site**, purely promotional sites included, with reported fines of roughly €250–€2,000 ([Diritto.it](https://www.diritto.it/l-indicazione-della-partita-iva-sul-sito-web-e-obbligatoria/)). The P.IVA, a real name and face, the area served, a direct phone or WhatsApp number, and links to a Google Business Profile or LinkedIn are all verifiable and none of them can be faked. Linking out signals confidence, because isolated sites seem to be hiding something ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/communicating-trustworthiness/)).

Several honest substitutes for testimonials have experimental support. **Voluntary cost transparency increased purchase interest through trust**, with up to about 20% more sales in the field study, but only when the disclosure was voluntary ([HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/15-017_f67df7f5-8336-47fc-a0c1-098838aa550d.pdf); [INFORMS](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/New-Research-Finds-Cost-Transparency-Can-Increase-Sales-20)). NN/g found **pricing was business buyers' top information need**. When prices were hidden, participants went to competitors, and NN/g recommends publishing starting prices or prices for common scenarios for custom services ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/); [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-prices-for-common-scenarios/)). Showing the work being done raises perceived value. This is the **"labor illusion" across five experiments** ([INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376)), and operational transparency has not been found to undermine trust ([HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Buell_Porter_Norton_01262020_WP_1c84113c-821c-4443-bfb6-3679722343a1.pdf)). Money-back guarantees reduce perceived risk most for experience goods, whose quality is hard to judge in advance ([Journal of Retailing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022435911000820)), and a website redesign is one. Believability, however, falls when an unfamiliar seller makes a very generous claim ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286120810_Do_Consumers_Perceive_Money-Back_Guarantees_as_Believable_The_Effects_of_Money-Back_Guarantee_Generosity_Store_Name_Familiarity_and_Perceived_Price)). A narrow offer such as "se l'anteprima non ti convince, non paghi nulla" is more credible than a sweeping guarantee. Responsiveness is a differentiator in its own right. An HBR audit found **23% of companies never answered a web lead** ([Venture Harbour, secondary](https://ventureharbour.com/how-fast-do-marketing-leads-turn-cold-and-how-to-stop-it-happening/)), so a response-time promise the founder can keep speaks directly to the buyer's fear of being ignored.

Owner-side surveys on hiring web designers do not exist in the notes. Practitioner sources consistently cite **hidden or recurring costs, loss of control over the domain and access, and unresponsiveness** as the fears to defuse ([Build 76](https://www.build76.co.uk/blog/hidden-costs-of-hiring-a-web-designer)). Italian SMEs lean more than the EU average on word of mouth (35% against 27%) when sourcing suppliers ([Il Giornale della Logistica](https://ilgiornaledellalogistica.it/trend/geopost-business-barometer-pmi/)). The data comes from logistics, but it suggests personal accessibility and a description easy to forward on WhatsApp will matter. The person behind the site should be singular and visible. No controlled study compares "I" with "we," but NN/g and Stanford both favour disclosing the real people ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)), and practitioners warn that a solo operator posing as a team looks dishonest once discovered ([UK Business Forums](https://www.ukbusinessforums.co.uk/threads/1-person-companies-we-or-i.397918/)).

The portfolio needs the same honesty. NN/g's survey of 204 hiring managers found **reviewers scan rather than read, and want 3–5 case studies that start from the problem and show role, reasoning and impact** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)). Agency-selection guides list cookie-cutter templates and missing mobile examples as red flags ([Utsubo](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/choose-web-design-agency-guide)). With no clients, sector-specific concept projects are the most persuasive thing available. They should be labelled "Progetto dimostrativo" and structured as problem, decisions and result, and they should report verifiable build facts such as page weight, Lighthouse score and accessibility checks rather than invented KPIs. The legal floor is explicit. The EU Omnibus Directive, transposed in Italy by D.Lgs. 26/2023, **bans fake or manipulated reviews** ([Il Sole 24 Ore](https://ntplusdiritto.ilsole24ore.com/art/la-direttiva-omnibus-nuove-regole-poste-tutela-consumatori-AEsGCvGD)). Buyers are already primed for suspicion: **8.6% of Italian reviews in 2024 were fake and 66% of consumers regularly meet unreliable ones** ([AGI](https://www.agi.it/economia/news/2025-06-15/recensioni-online-impatto-falsita-proposta-normativa-31875921/)).

## One goal, short forms and WhatsApp lower the cost of enquiring

Conversion frameworks agree on what turns interest into an enquiry. MECLABS's heuristic, **C = 4m + 3v + 2(i − f) − 2a**, weights the visitor's motivation most heavily, then value-proposition clarity, and subtracts friction and anxiety ([MarketingExperiments](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/10-heuristic-cheat-sheet)). The coefficients are expert judgment drawn from experiments, not a fitted model. Anxiety fixes should sit close to where the concern arises, for example "niente spam" next to the phone field ([MarketingExperiments](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/friction-and-anxiety-in-your-marketing-process-defining-the-difference)). An Unbounce analysis of 18,639 landing pages reported **13.5% conversion for single-CTA pages against 10.5% for pages with many**. The source is secondary and about 2018 ([MarketingProfs](https://www.marketingprofs.com/charts/2018/33935/what-converts-web-landing-page-benchmarks-and-trends-infographic)). Repeating one goal at natural decision points is not the same as offering competing actions. No controlled study proves a single hero CTA beats several, so this rests on consistent practitioner evidence.

Forms are where friction becomes measurable. HubSpot's analysis of 40,000+ landing pages found that **text areas, drop-downs and age and phone fields lowered conversion**, while single-line fields did not ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/blog/tabid/6307/bid/6746/which-types-of-form-fields-lower-landing-page-conversions.aspx)). Baymard found users hesitate to give phone numbers, and some abandon or enter false data when the field is required without explanation ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/explain-phone-number-field)). Three or four fields is the right target: name, business or current site URL, preferred contact, and an optional message. Italy offers a lower-friction path. **WhatsApp reaches 90.1% of Italian internet users** ([Agenda Digitale](https://www.agendadigitale.eu/mercati-digitali/whatsapp-nelle-aziende-italiane-come-cambia-il-rapporto-coi-clienti/)), and many target businesses already run on it. A prefilled WhatsApp link and tap-to-call, shown consistently as the secondary route, probably cut more friction than any form tweak, though no Italian experiment confirms it. CTA copy should name a concrete, low-risk outcome and what happens next. Pronoun-swap "hacks" are page-specific, and one review of 250+ tests found rewording wins about one time in eight ([Review42](https://resources.review42.com/call-to-action-tips/)). Plain language helps. Unbounce data reportedly shows simpler copy converting better, and restaurant and catering pages have the simplest copy of any vertical ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report-2020/); [Shno, secondary](https://www.shno.co/marketing-statistics/landing-page-conversion-statistics)).

## Motion earns its place by explaining, never by hijacking

Motion attracts attention, which is exactly why it competes with the headline and the CTA. NN/g finds that **elements that slide or move capture attention faster than fades and can pull readers off the main content**, and that most UI transitions should run about 100–500 ms ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-usability/); [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)). The legitimate uses are feedback, state changes and showing where something went. A before/after makeover qualifies, because it explains the very transformation the studio sells. A living WebGL background, a marquee ticker or a 3D phone that rotates only for show is decoration that competes for attention.

Taking control of the scroll is the clearest red line. NN/g's usability testing found that **scrolljacking poses "significant threats to user control and freedom, discoverability, attention, efficiency, and task success"**. It distinguishes scrolljacking from scroll fading, which triggers animations without changing scroll behaviour and is acceptable if used sparingly ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/); [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scroll-fading-101/)). Mandatory scroll-snap combined with 100vh scenes doubles the risk, because each scene is also a potential false floor. Parallax can cause dizziness even in people without vestibular disorders ([Princeton](https://digital.accessibility.princeton.edu/how/content/animations)). Auto-forwarding content annoys users ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/auto-forwarding/)), and moving content lasting more than five seconds needs a pause control under WCAG 2.2.2 ([TestParty](https://testparty.ai/blog/accessible-carousels-sliders-auto-playing-media)). Reduced-motion support is not an edge case for this audience. **35.4% of US adults aged 40 and over have measurable vestibular dysfunction, rising from 18.5% in their 40s** ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19468085)).

Interactive "show, don't tell" assets have only correlational evidence behind them. Vendor data puts conversion among demo-engaged visitors at about 8x the average ([Storylane](https://www.storylane.io/blog/interactive-demo-statistics-2026)), but motivated visitors self-select into engaging. Two figures are more useful: **ungated demos were used by about 24% of visitors, and demos placed higher on the page got 3.5x the engagement** ([Navattic](https://www.navattic.com/blog/increase-conversions-for-interactive-demos)). An interactive before/after or a scrollable demo inside a phone frame belongs near the top. A passive autoplay animation is closer to a hero video, where the evidence is mixed ([MetricTheory](https://metrictheory.com/blog/how-a-performance-marketing-agency-tests-our-own-website/)). Award juries judge design, usability, creativity and content, with **no conversion criterion** ([Digidop](https://www.digidop.com/blog/complete-guide-awwards-digital-agencies)), so Awwwards-style immersion proves craft to peers but not effectiveness with a B&B owner. Commentators on 2026 winners note a shift toward one strong concept rather than stacked effects ([Digital Strategy Force](https://digitalstrategyforce.com/journal/why-are-immersive-experiences-dominating-the-2026-awwwards/)). That is the right model here: one signature, purposeful interaction, with the ambient layers cut or deferred.

## Phones and milliseconds set the ceiling on everything else

The audience is on phones. Audiweb measured **88–89% of Italians' online time on mobile in 2025, and mobile reached about 95% of 18–74-year-olds** ([Touchpoint News](https://www.touchpoint.news/2025/12/17/audiweb-a-ottobre-2025-lonline-coinvolge-441-milioni-di-italiani/); [ADC Group](https://www.adcgroup.it/adv-express/big-data/audiweb/audiweb-ad-aprile-sono-stati-44-5-milioni-gli-individui-online-pari-al-76-4-della-popolazione.html)). StatCounter's near 50/50 split counts page views on tracked sites and over-weights desk browsing ([StatCounter](https://gs.statcounter.com/platform-market-share/desktop-mobile-tablet/italy)). An owner is most likely to first meet the studio through a link opened on a phone. Mobile reading punishes complexity. NN/g measured comprehension of difficult text on a phone at 48% of desktop in 2011, and later found short, simple text is understood equally well on both ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content-is-twice-as-difficult-2011/); [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/)). **49% of people hold the phone one-handed** ([UXmatters](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2013/02/how-do-users-really-hold-mobile-devices.php)), which favours primary actions in the lower thumb zone. Tap targets should be at least about 1 cm, or 44–48 CSS px with spacing ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/); [Digital.gov](https://digital.gov/guides/mobile-principles/tap-targets)). A sticky bottom "Scrivimi su WhatsApp" bar fits these findings, as long as it does not cover content.

Speed has the strongest quantitative evidence in this whole brief. Deloitte's Google-commissioned study of 37 brands found that **a 0.1 s mobile speed gain was associated with 8.4% more retail conversions, and with an 8.3% bounce improvement on lead-generation pages**, the closest analogue to a studio ([Deloitte](https://deloitte.com/ie/en/services/consulting/research/milliseconds-make-millions); [web.dev](https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions)). The study is observational. Google's mobile landing-page data shows **bounce probability rising 32% as load time goes from 1 s to 3 s** ([Think with Google](https://thinkwithgoogle.com/data/page-load-time-statistics)). The most relevant result is an Italian A/B test: **Vodafone Italy's 31% better LCP produced 8% more sales** on visually identical pages ([web.dev](https://web.dev/case-studies/vitals-business-impact)). The baseline web gives no headroom. Median mobile Total Blocking Time reached **1,916 ms in 2025, nearly ten times the 200 ms "good" threshold**, and only 48% of mobile origins pass all Core Web Vitals ([Web Almanac 2025](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/performance)). A homepage that layers GSAP timelines, a WebGL shader and a 3D model starts behind on a mid-range Android. The practical pattern is to render the hero as static HTML and an image so LCP never waits on a canvas, initialise WebGL and 3D after idle or first interaction, pause them off-screen, and serve a static fallback under `prefers-reduced-motion`.

## Conclusion

The evidence reframes what "diving in" means for this kind of site. It is less about delight than about the visitor's risk reduction happening in sequence. The 50 ms judgment rewards a page that looks like a credible, ordinary studio site done unusually well. The ten-second read rewards an offer an owner can repeat to a spouse. Every scroll after that is the owner checking whether a real person will answer, charge what was stated and hand over the keys. For a studio with no clients, the most persuasive "wow" is not an effect. It is a sector-specific before/after that is fast on a phone, framed by a founder's face, a P.IVA and a price. Two strong traditions pull against each other here, award-style craft and conversion-led restraint, and the resolution is to spend craft on the proof rather than the packaging.

Several things remain uncertain and are worth testing rather than assuming. No study shows that the template or AI look lowers trust. No research covers wide display type or 3D device mockups. The one-CTA advantage and the WhatsApp-over-form hunch both lack controlled evidence for Italian local services. And no survey exists of how Italian micro-business owners actually choose a web designer. Five-second tests and a handful of recorded sessions with real B&B and renovation-firm owners would answer more of these questions than any further desk research. The checklist below is built to show where those tests should focus.

## A weighted 100-point checklist for scoring the site (synthesis)

**This checklist is a synthesis, not a validated or published instrument.** No framework publishes empirically validated weights for a 100-point website score ([MarketingExperiments](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/10-heuristic-cheat-sheet); [Utsubo on Awwwards](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/award-winning-website-design-guide)). The weights below were set by judgment and anchored to published ones. MECLABS weights motivation and value clarity above friction and anxiety (4m, 3v, 2f, 2a). Awwwards weights Design 40, Usability 30, Creativity 20 and Content 10. HubSpot's Website Grader gives performance and mobile 60 of 100 ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/website-grader)). Lighthouse weights TBT 30, LCP 25 and CLS 25. In Stanford's data, design look accounts for 46.1% of credibility comments.

Two adjustments fit this particular case. Trust and anxiety reduction are weighted up (18 points in block F, plus 5 more for founder and provenance imagery in block E) because the studio has no clients and must replace borrowed reputation with verifiable transparency. Creativity and "wow" are weighted down to a single point, because award juries do not judge conversion and the buyers are not designers.

Grouped by purpose, the blocks give 24 points to value and relevance (A + B, the MECLABS m and v), 26 to visual design and imagery (C + D + E, Fogg and the Awwwards Design share), 18 to trust and proof (F), 10 to conversion friction (G), and 22 to motion, mobile and speed (H + I + J, Awwwards Usability, HubSpot and Lighthouse).

**Scoring rules.** Award full marks only when the "full marks" condition holds on both a 375 px phone and a desktop viewport. Award roughly half when it holds on one of them or only partly. Award zero otherwise. Three gates override the arithmetic. Any fabricated testimonial, client logo, review or KPI, or any concept work presented as client work, sets block F to zero. A missing Partita IVA on the homepage caps block F at 10. Any mandatory scroll-snap or scrolljacking caps block H at 3.

**Reading the score.** A site scoring 85 or more with no gate triggered should be making its case to owners well, and the remaining gains will come from testing with real prospects. Between 65 and 84, look first at blocks A and F, which carry the heaviest evidence for this audience. Below 65, or with any gate triggered, fix the fundamentals (offer clarity, verifiable trust, native scroll, mobile speed) before adding any further visual refinement.
