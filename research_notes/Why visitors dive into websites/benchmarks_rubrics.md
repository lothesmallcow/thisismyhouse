# Benchmark small-studio websites and existing website scoring rubrics

Method note: in this environment, direct page fetches were blocked by the egress proxy. nngroup.com, cxl.com, meclabs.com, manyrequests.com and designjoy.co all returned "connect_rejected". So every finding below comes from web-search result summaries and snippets, not full reads of the pages. Where a figure comes from a third-party summary and not the primary source, it is flagged. The report writer should treat studio-specific details (prices, tiers, award dates) as "reported by X" rather than as verified.

## Q1. What do the strongest small-agency homepages put in the first screen, and how do they mix personality with professionalism? (Includes examples, layout patterns and pricing pages)

### Takeaway
In 2025-2026 the strong agency first screens fall into three archetypes:
- a text-led statement hero (one big headline plus one or two sentences);
- a full-bleed showreel or rotating featured project;
- a hover or interactive hero that reveals work.

All three keep a visible contact path ("Let's talk") and put proof (awards, logos, results) right under the fold.

Productized studios aimed at small businesses replace the artsy hero with a plain offer: a price, a timeline ("live in 14 days") and a guarantee. Personality comes from voice and one signature interaction, not from hiding the offer.

### Cited Findings

It cites Fantasy and Huge as examples of rotating featured projects in the hero — [Muffin Group, Best agency websites](https://muffingroup.com/blog/best-agency-websites/)
- Collins (a brand consultancy) compresses a complex offer into one headline and two sentences in its hero — [Teamwork, 10 best marketing agency websites 2026](https://www.teamwork.com/blog/marketing-agency-websites/)

- Plusdrie's showreel grows as you scroll and becomes full-screen when clicked, with a "Let's talk" button that stays visible. For the People's headline reveals client work as the cursor moves over it. Dataland stacks full-width case cards vertically — [Framer blog: 11 best marketing agency websites in 2026 (mirror)](https://most-exercise-922671--qsphqoa58.framer.app/blog/marketing-agency-websites/)
- Hero videos, animated case studies and micro-interactions "hold attention long enough to convert". HigherVisibility and KlientBoost show impact with dashboards and visual metrics — [DesignRush, Best advertising agency websites 2026](https://www.designrush.com/agency/ad-agencies/trends/top-advertising-agency-websites)

- In the 2026 teardowns, the most consistent lessons are a strong first screen, visible contact paths, and case studies with measurable results. Several of these roundups are sponsored or SEO-driven — same search across [Colorlib](https://colorlib.com/wp/digital-agency-website-examples/), [InspoAI](https://www.inspoai.io/blogs/creative-agency-website-examples), [ClientManager](https://www.clientmanager.io/blog/agency-websites-inspiration)

**Why the first 10 seconds decide**
- NN/g (Nielsen), drawing on a 2010 Microsoft Research study (Liu, White, Dumais), found that the first 10 seconds of a page visit are when leaving is most likely. Visitors stay highly likely to leave over the next ~20 seconds, and the curve flattens after ~30 seconds. Nielsen's recommendation: "to gain several minutes of user attention, you must clearly communicate your value proposition within 10 seconds." — [NN/g, How long do users stay on web pages?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/). The popular "10-20 seconds" wording is a secondary paraphrase, not verbatim NN/g.
- Stanford Web Credibility Research (B.J. Fogg; 2,684 participants, 100 sites): "design look" was the top credibility factor, mentioned in 46.1% of comments. Information design/structure came second (28.5%) and information focus third (25.1%) — [Design for Hackers summary](https://designforhackers.com/?p=413); [ClickZ, "For site cred, looks matter"](https://clickz.com/study-for-site-cred-looks-matter/56564/)

### Inferences
- Two different benchmarks exist, and the target site probably needs a hybrid:
  - **Award-gallery studios** (Adoratorio, Malvah, Kota, Collins) sell craft to marketing directors. Their first screen is a statement or showreel, and the offer is implied.
  - **Small-business studios** (Neon, Internet Crafters, SiteReadyWeb, Designjoy) sell certainty to owners. Their first screen states price, speed and a guarantee.

  For a studio selling to local businesses: an award-gallery look for credibility (Fogg's 46%), with an offer-first headline for the 10-second test (NN/g).
- The way to show personality without losing professionalism, seen across examples: keep one signature interaction (Plusdrie's growing reel, For the People's hover-reveal) and a distinctive voice. Keep the offer, CTA and proof conventional and always visible.
- Pricing-page pattern among productized studios: one to three tiers, a flat price, a timeline, explicit inclusions, and a risk reverser ("pay only if you approve", "five mockups"). This is inferred from the examples above. I could not read a pricing page in full.

- I found no Italian studio that both wins design awards and publishes small-business price packages. Search returned only generic Italian cost guides and PMI-focused vendors without prices (e.g., Visione Reale).
- Official Awwwards pages confirming the Malvah 2025 title and Adoratorio's Studio of the Year year were not read directly. The Malvah article is dated June 2026.

## Q2. How do studios show work when it is limited (spec projects, concept work, process stories)?

### Takeaway
The consensus advice is:
- Show 3-5 deep case studies rather than many thumbnails.
- Structure each as problem → process → result.
- Openly use unsolicited redesigns of real local businesses, self-initiated projects and pro-bono or discounted work.

The test for each piece is "if a client could only see this one project, would they hire me?"

### Cited Findings
- Treat a real business with an outdated site as a practice client: redesign it and present it as a full case study — [Contra, Building a winning freelance web design portfolio](https://contra.com/p/cr2OMqR7-building-a-winning-freelance-web-design-portfolio-tips-and-examples)
- Build self-initiated website ideas; the finished project proves you can handle that type of work — [WebdesignerDepot, How to build a design portfolio with zero clients](https://www.webdesignerdepot.com/2022/10/how-to-build-a-design-portfolio-with-zero-clients/)
- Case-study structure: problem, research, wireframes, iterations, final design, measurable outcomes. Alternatively: summary, problem, process, and what worked or didn't and why — [Medium, UX portfolio without client work](https://medium.com/@kellyleigh/creating-a-standout-ux-portfolio-without-client-work-ba84edd223d4); [Ladnatech 2026, problem-process-result method](https://www.ladnatech.com/2026/08/how-to-build-graphic-design-portfolio-no-clients.html)
- Feature only 3-5 of your strongest case studies. Ask: "If a client could only see this one project, would they hire me?" — [Cocoon](https://cocoon.agency/post/creating-a-great-web-design-portfolio-no-experience); [Contra](https://contra.com/p/cr2OMqR7-building-a-winning-freelance-web-design-portfolio-tips-and-examples)
- Discounted or pro-bono work for real organizations builds reputation and referrals. Sources are cautious about unpaid work for friends — [ThinkToShare](https://thinktoshare.com/blogs/build-design-portfolio-without-experience); [DEV/Ramotion](https://dev.to/ramotiondev/creating-a-web-design-portfolio-without-work-experience-2ff5)
- Agency teardowns favor case studies with measurable results and visual metrics (dashboards) over pure visuals — [DesignRush](https://www.designrush.com/agency/ad-agencies/trends/top-advertising-agency-websites)

### Inferences
- For a small-business studio, a "before/after makeover" of a real local business works as a spec case study. It shows the problem (the old site), the process (the diagnosis) and the result (the new site, plus projected impact), and non-designer owners can read it immediately. Labelling it honestly as a concept or redesign preserves trust (the Fogg credibility factors include making information verifiable).
- Interactive "live demo" frames (a scrollable site inside a phone or browser) let concept work be experienced rather than just viewed. That partly makes up for missing client logos and metrics. This is an inference; I found no study on it.

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative data on whether labelled concept work converts worse than client work with prospects.
- There is no source on how award-winning studios present early-stage work (e.g., "Lab" or "Experiments" sections).

## Q3. What common mistakes do designer and agency portfolio sites make that hurt conversion with non-designer clients?

### Takeaway
The recurring failures are:
- the site as a showpiece for the designer rather than a sales tool;
- a vague headline or offer that names no outcome;
- many competing CTAs;
- low-quality or irrelevant work samples;
- slow or poor mobile performance;
- motion that takes scroll control away from the user (scrolljacking).

Award juries themselves weight usability at 30%, and commentators say that is where most submissions fail.

### Cited Findings
- The designer's site acts as a showpiece rather than carrying the material prospects need to decide whether to hire — [The Little Design Corner, 5 big mistakes designers make on their website](https://thelittledesigncorner.com/blog/website-mistakes)
- Vague headlines give visitors no reason to stay, and bounce rises when the headline names no outcome. Generic button labels ("Submit", "Learn more") are said to underperform outcome-specific labels (weak source) — [PulseRevOps](https://pulserevops.com/software/sw263)
- Too many competing CTAs dilute attention; the fix is one primary action per page — [DEV, 10 UI/UX mistakes that hurt conversions](https://dev.to/pixel_mosaic/10-uiux-mistakes-that-hurt-website-conversions-and-how-to-fix-them-ck2)
- Low-resolution or inconsistent images, outdated styles and work irrelevant to the target client: viewers judge whether a portfolio looks serious in under a second — [Dribbble, 5 design portfolio mistakes](https://dribbble.com/stories/2021/05/18/5-design-portfolio-mistakes-that-ll-send-clients-running-in-the-wrong-direction)
- Not built for phones; booking prompts or chatbots appearing constantly during scrolling feel like pressure — [Astute](https://astute.co/resource/website-design-mistakes-that-are-killing-your-conversions/)
- Slow load times and mobile neglect are "foundational failures" — [Roya](https://www.roya.com/blog/is-your-website-costing-you-leads-5-mistakes-that-are-hurting-conversions.html)
- Scrolljacking breaks "one of the oldest agreements in web UX". It penalizes users who skim, scan and navigate fast, often breaks on older hardware and assistive tech, and stutters when scripts force repaints — [WebdesignerDepot, How scrolljacking breaks UX fundamentals](https://webdesignerdepot.com/how-scrolljacking-breaks-ux-fundamentals/)
- People with vestibular disorders or who use keyboard navigation can be blocked or disoriented when motion is not optional — [Alvaro Trigo, What is scroll hijacking](https://alvarotrigo.com/blog/what-is-scroll-hijacking/)
- Recommended mitigations: let users control scroll speed and freely revisit content. When users don't scroll far, the cause is more often weak content or messaging than the scroll mechanic — [Robin Rendle, scrolljacking notes](https://robinrendle.com/notes/scrolljacking); [HubSpot, Scrolljacking: the good, the bad, the ugly](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/scrolljacking-the-good-the-bad-the-ugly); [nclud, Scrolljacking the less bad way](https://sketchbook.nclud.com/scrolljacking/)
- Awwwards weights Usability at 30%: navigation, clarity, performance, responsiveness, accessibility. One commentator argues usability (including Core Web Vitals) is where most submissions fail. That is an opinion, not an Awwwards finding — [Utsubo](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/award-winning-website-design-guide); [hontran.dev](https://www.hontran.dev/blog/awwwards-judging-criteria)
- Unbounce benchmarks: the median landing page converts at 6.6% across industries (2024 report). In 2020 the median was 3.2% and the top quartile 25%. Restaurant and catering pages had the simplest copy, SaaS pages the hardest to read. The years are not directly comparable — [Unbounce 2024 report](https://unbounce.com/?p=94295); [Unbounce 2020 report](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report-2020/)

### Inferences
- For non-designer local-business clients, the biggest risk of an "artsy" studio site is not that it looks too designed. Fogg says design look builds credibility. The risk is that personality replaces the offer (Little Design Corner) and that motion takes control away (scrolljacking). Full-screen snap or "slides" homepages are a form of scrolljacking and carry the same risks.
- Plain, simple copy suits this audience: per Unbounce, the restaurant and catering verticals use the simplest copy.

### Gaps
- I could not access NN/g's own scrolljacking article (proxy-blocked) to quote its exact recommendations. It exists (nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/), but its contents here are unverified.
- I found no controlled study quantifying conversion loss from scrolljacking specifically.
- I found no Unbounce data on page speed vs. conversion in the results returned.

## Q4. Which weighted criteria do established conversion and UX audit frameworks use, and how are they weighted?

### Takeaway
Only a few frameworks publish explicit numeric weights:
- **MECLABS:** C = 4m + 3v + 2(i−f) − 2a
- **Lighthouse Performance:** TBT 30, LCP 25, CLS 25, FCP 10, SI 10
- **HubSpot Website Grader:** Performance 30, SEO 30, Mobile 30, Security 10
- **Awwwards:** Design 40, Usability 30, Creativity 20, Content 10
- **CSS Design Awards:** UI 40, UX 30, Innovation 30

LIFT, CXL/ResearchXL and Nielsen's 10 heuristics are unweighted checklists. Any 100-point weighting of them is a design choice, not a standard.

### Cited Findings
**MECLABS / MarketingExperiments Conversion Sequence Heuristic**
- C = 4m + 3v + 2(i − f) − 2a, where:
  - C = probability of conversion;
  - m = motivation of the user (the heaviest weight);
  - v = clarity of the value proposition ("why should I buy from you rather than anyone else");
  - i = incentive;
  - f = friction (psychological resistance, with two components: length and difficulty);
  - a = anxiety (psychological concern triggered by a step).

  The coefficients express relative impact. MECLABS says the heuristic was derived from experiments on 20,000+ sales paths over about 20 years. It is a patented heuristic, not a fitted statistical model — [MarketingExperiments, 10-heuristic cheat sheet](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/10-heuristic-cheat-sheet); [MarketingExperiments, friction vs anxiety](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/friction-and-anxiety-in-your-marketing-process-defining-the-difference); [MarketingExperiments, customer anxiety](https://marketingexperiments.com/value-proposition/customer-anxiety-conversion-heuristic); [Frank Hopkins, The Conversion Sequence Heuristic](https://frankphopkins.medium.com/the-conversion-sequence-heuristic-3d476b959713); [MarketingSherpa](https://www.marketingsherpa.com/article/other/where-to-test-video)
- MarketingExperiments advises reducing friction but not eliminating necessary fields. Anxiety fixes should be specific, close in time and place to where the concern arises, and strong enough to counter it — [MarketingExperiments](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/friction-and-anxiety-in-your-marketing-process-defining-the-difference)
- A variant, "C = 4m + i + v/a", appears on SaaSHero. It is an altered form and should not be used — [SaaSHero](https://www.saashero.net/strategy/heuristic-analysis-works-conversion-optimization/)

**WiderFunnel / Conversion.com LIFT Model** (Chris Goward, 2009; Landing page Influence Function for Tests)
- There are six factors. Value Proposition sits at the center: the cost-vs-benefit equation that taps the prospect's motivation. Five factors act on it:
  - drivers: Relevance, Clarity, Urgency;
  - inhibitors: Anxiety, Distraction.

  It is mainly used to generate A/B test hypotheses, and no numeric weights are published — [Conversion.com, The LIFT Model](https://conversion.com/?p=4613); [Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/lift-model-for-conversion-value-relevance-clarity-anxiety-distraction-urgency/); [Mention Me](https://blog.mention-me.com/blog/6-conversion-optimisation-tips-using-lift-model)

**CXL ResearchXL heuristic analysis**
- Pages are walked through for:
  - Relevance: does the page match the visitor's expectations and intent?
  - Clarity: can you tell what it is and who it's for within about 5 seconds?
  - Value: how does the visitor benefit?
  - Friction: psychological resistance in the process.
  - Distraction: "If it's not motivation, it's friction."

  Some versions add anxiety, motivation and buying stage. The findings are "areas of interest" to validate with qualitative and quantitative research, not scores — [CXL, How to come up with more winning tests (ResearchXL)](https://cxl.com/blog/how-to-come-up-with-more-winning-tests-using-data/); [Speero (CXL agency) on ResearchXL](https://speero.com/post/how-to-create-winning-tests-using-the-researchxl-model); [Gorohovsky course notes](https://gorohovsky.com/researchxl-conversion-optimization-course-notes/); [LinkedIn, Heuristic analysis part 2](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/all-heuristic-analysis-conversion-make-your-part-2-mahmud)

**Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics**
- No canonical weighting is published. Common practice is to score each heuristic 0–4 (40 points max) or 1–5. Some audit frameworks score each of the 10 on 1–10 to reach a "/100" score. Issue severity (P0 blocking to P2 minor) is kept separate from the score — [impeccable heuristics-scoring guide](https://git.secluded.site/agent-skills/blob/main/vendor/impeccable/skill/reference/heuristics-scoring.md); [UX audit framework](https://sharedcontext.ai/skills/external/WilliamFontaine/ux-audit-framework); [RGD, Heuristic analysis in UX audits](https://rgd.ca/articles/heuristic-analysis-in-ux-audits)

**Lighthouse performance score**
- Lighthouse 10+ weights: TBT 30%, LCP 25%, CLS 25%, FCP 10%, Speed Index 10%. TTI was removed. The history is in [What's new in Lighthouse 6.0](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/lighthouse-6-0): v6 had TBT 25, LCP 25, FCP 15, SI 15, TTI 15, CLS 5. The current split comes from third-party calculators and was not read on Google's docs — [Unlighthouse calculator](https://unlighthouse.dev/tools/lighthouse-score-calculator); [DeployHQ guide](https://pagespeed.deployhq.com/guides/lighthouse-score)

**HubSpot Website Grader**
- The categories add up to 100: Performance 30, SEO 30, Mobile 30, Security 10. This is inferred from an example dashboard showing 14/30, 30/30, 30/30 and 10/10.
- Each category checks:
  - Performance: page size, requests, speed, caching;
  - SEO: indexing, meta descriptions, descriptive link text;
  - Mobile: responsive design, legible font size, tap targets, viewport meta;
  - Security: HTTPS, secure JS libraries, mixed content.
- Across 1M+ submissions, the average grade is 60.9/100 and the average performance score 18/30 — [HubSpot blog, Website Grader](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/website-grader); [HubSpot Research, Does your website make the grade?](https://research.hubspot.com/reports/does-your-website-make-the-grade); [Pedowitz Group](https://pedowitzgroup.com/website-grader?hsLang=en)

**Awwwards jury**
- Design 40%, Usability 30%, Creativity 20%, Content 10%, judged by 18 or more jurors. The scores furthest from the mean are discarded.
- Thresholds conflict across sources: about 6.5 for an Honorable Mention vs about 8.0 for SOTD consideration. An older rubric had six criteria, including mobile and developer.
- CSS Design Awards uses UI 40 / UX 30 / Innovation 30 — [Utsubo](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/award-winning-website-design-guide); [hontran.dev, Awwwards judging criteria](https://www.hontran.dev/blog/awwwards-judging-criteria); [Shakuro 2021](https://shakuro.com/blog/best-website-designs-of-2021-and-what-they-have-in-common)

**Credibility (Stanford/Fogg)**
- Design look 46.1%, information design/structure 28.5%, information focus 25.1% (share of credibility comments, not weights). Fogg's 10 guidelines include "make it easy to verify the accuracy of information"; the full list was not retrieved — [Design for Hackers](https://designforhackers.com/?p=413); [makinggood.ac.nz, web credibility](https://makinggood.ac.nz/methods/web-credibility/)

### Inferences
Here is a proposed 100-point weighted checklist synthesized from the frameworks above. The weights are my own and derived as follows:
- MECLABS ranks motivation/value above friction and anxiety.
- Awwwards puts Design + Usability at 70%.
- HubSpot gives performance + mobile 60%.
- Fogg finds design look and structure dominate credibility.

| Block | Pts | Derived from |
|---|---|---|
| A. Value proposition and clarity in the first screen: what, for whom, outcome, price or time cue, 5-10 s test | 20 | MECLABS v (×3), LIFT center, CXL clarity, NN/g 10 s |
| B. Relevance and motivation match: speaks the local-business owner's language, problem/outcome framing | 10 | MECLABS m (×4), LIFT/CXL relevance |
| C. Visual design and craft: hierarchy, type, colour, consistency, distinctive personality | 15 | Awwwards Design 40%, Fogg 46% |
| D. Proof and anxiety reduction: case studies with results, before/after, testimonials, guarantees, real people, address | 15 | MECLABS −2a, LIFT anxiety, Fogg verifiability |
| E. Friction and CTA: one primary CTA, visible contact path, short form, outcome-labelled buttons, transparent pricing | 10 | MECLABS −2f, CXL friction, one-CTA advice |
| F. Distraction and focus: no competing CTAs, no gratuitous motion, no scrolljacking | 5 | LIFT distraction, CXL distraction |
| G. Usability (Nielsen-derived): navigation, consistency, user control and freedom, error prevention, readable copy | 10 | Nielsen 10, Awwwards Usability 30% |
| H. Performance: Lighthouse/CWV with LCP, CLS, TBT/INP | 8 | Lighthouse weights, HubSpot 30 |
| I. Mobile and accessibility: responsive layout, tap targets, contrast, reduced motion | 5 | HubSpot Mobile 30, WCAG |
| J. Engagement and curiosity: a signature interaction, content that invites exploring | 2 | Awwwards Creativity 20% (deliberately down-weighted for SMB buyers) |
| **Total** | **100** | |

- If a site is judged for "makes visitors want to explore" rather than pure conversion, shift 5-10 points from E/F toward C/J. This mirrors the Awwwards vs CRO split.
- Urgency (LIFT) and incentive (MECLABS i) matter little for a considered B2B service purchase like a website. They can fold into E (e.g., limited monthly slots, a launch offer).
