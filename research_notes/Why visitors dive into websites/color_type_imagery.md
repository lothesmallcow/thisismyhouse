## Color: what research (not pop psychology) says about trust, warmth and conversion; CTA color; saturation; colorfulness; orange; Italy

### Takeaway
The solid evidence is about **first-impression aesthetics, contrast and distinctiveness**, not about specific hues "causing" trust or sales. Visitors judge visual appeal in 50–500 ms, visual complexity matters more than colorfulness, and the CTA works when it is visually isolated from its surroundings (von Restorff), not because it is orange. Hue-to-personality links (red/orange = exciting, blue = competent) have some academic support, but they are about brand personality, not conversion. Saturation drives arousal/excitement. The site's orange #ff5b22 does **not** reach 4.5:1 against its own cream or with white text. It is fine only as a fill behind dark text, or for large display text.

### Cited Findings
**First impressions and colorfulness (solid)**
- Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown (2006, *Behaviour & Information Technology*): people form visual-appeal judgments of web pages from a 50 ms exposure, and these correlate highly with judgments made after longer viewing. A follow-up found that people also judge trustworthiness and usability quickly, "but it does take a bit longer." — [Blogcritics summary](https://blogcritics.org/users-judge-websites-almost-instantly/); [Human Factors Intl newsletter](https://humanfactors.com/newsletters/an_exploration_of_relations_between_visual_appeal.asp). Claims that this "halo" carries over to credibility and usability come from commentators, not from the paper's abstract. Treat them as interpretation.
- Reinecke et al. (CHI 2013): 450 websites were rated by 548 volunteers after 500 ms views. Computational models of colorfulness and visual complexity, plus demographics, explain about half the variance in appeal. "Colorfulness plays a role, but is not nearly as important as the overall visual complexity of a site." Age, gender and education all shift first impressions. — [Harvard paper page](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2013/reinecke13predicting.shtml); [PDF](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)
- Reinecke & Gajos (CHI 2014, "Quantifying Visual Preferences Around the World"): 2.4 million ratings from about 40,000 participants. Preferred colorfulness and complexity vary strongly by demographic, for example women liked colorful sites more than men. — [PDF](https://iis.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke14visual.pdf). I could not fetch the full paper to extract Italy- or country-specific numbers. In my recollection of the paper (unverified in this session), low-to-moderate complexity is generally preferred, and preferences differ by country. See Gaps.
- Stanford Web Credibility study (Fogg et al., 2002; 2,684 participants): "design look" was the most frequently mentioned credibility factor (46.1% of comments), ahead of information structure. It was highest for finance sites (54.6%). Fogg: "to look good is to be good." — [Consumer Reports writeup](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility); [Internet News](https://internetnews.com/marketing/study-for-site-cred-looks-matter); [CMSWire](https://www.cmswire.com/cms/featured-articles/people-found-to-be-overwhelmingly-superficial-000114.php). Caveat: outlets disagree on whether 46.1% is a share of comments or of participants. The comments reading (Consumer Reports) is more likely correct. Some marketers misquote it as "46% say design is their primary factor" ([LeanLabs](https://www.leanlabs.com/faq/b2b-websites/how-do-buyers-judge-a-companys-legitimacy-based-on-website-design)), which overstates it.

**Hue, saturation, brand personality (moderate)**
- Labrecque & Milne, "Exciting red and competent blue" (J. Academy of Marketing Science, 2012, 40(5):711–727). Four studies: hues map onto brand-personality dimensions (red to excitement, blue to competence); saturation and value amplify traits; color can shift brand personality and purchase intent; color affects likability and familiarity. — [abstract](https://colab.ws/articles/10.1007%2Fs11747-010-0245-y). Secondary summary: saturation is linked to excitement, value (lightness) to robustness ([UNL thesis](https://run.unl.pt/entities/publication/276bb2e1-b4f7-4bdb-b40c-be90464c9a85/full)).
- A CBS master's thesis replication confirmed blue logos read as competent and red as exciting. It also found that following product-category color norms gives more positive brand attitudes. — [CBS thesis](https://research.cbs.dk/en/studentProjects/competent-blue-and-exciting-red-examining-the-effect-of-logo-colo/)
- Gorn & Chattopadhyay et al. (1990s ad research): higher chroma (saturation) raised arousal; higher value (lightness) raised relaxation. — [IIMB working paper](https://repository.iimb.ac.in/handle/123456789/521)
- Orange specifically: I found **no primary research result for orange alone**. Grouping orange with red as "exciting" appears only in uncited blogs ([editing.services](https://editing.services/blog/the-psychology-of-color-6-logo-color-secrets-you-should-know)). Weak evidence.

**CTA/button color (myth vs mechanism)**
- "Best button color" tests (red beats green and so on) measure **contrast with the surrounding page**, not hue. Color in isolation is not a meaningful test variable. Test contrast or visual weight instead. — [Atticus Li](https://atticusli.com/blog/posts/cta-button-ab-tests-why-button-color-tests-are-worthless/); [EyeQuant](https://www.eyequant.com/blog/button-color-on-websites). Practitioner sources, but the reasoning is consistent with the von Restorff isolation effect: an item that differs from its surroundings is noticed and remembered more ([Switas glossary](https://www.switas.com/glossary/von-restorff-effect)).
- A CXL analysis is reported as finding orange, blue, red and green the most common colors among 90 high-converting CTAs. That shows prevalence, not causation, and I could only find it secondhand. — [Popupsmart citing CXL](https://popupsmart.com/blog/best-cta-button-color)

**Contrast requirements (standard)**
- WCAG 2.x SC 1.4.3: text needs 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text (at least 24px regular or 18.66px bold). SC 1.4.11: UI components and their boundaries need 3:1. — [W3C Understanding 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html); [W3C Understanding 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)
- Ratios I computed for the site's tokens:
  - #ff5b22 on carta #fff8f2 = **2.95:1**. This fails for any text, including large text, and is just under the 3:1 non-text minimum. Orange outlines, icons or links on cream are a problem.
  - White on #ff5b22 = **3.1:1**. Large text only, so a white-label orange button with 16px text fails.
  - Inchiostro #24130c on #ff5b22 = **5.77:1**. Passes, which matches the design note in the CSS ("cacao text on it").
  - #ff5b22 on nebbia #fcebdc = 2.67:1. Fails.
  - Grafite #6b5248 on carta = 6.82:1 and on nebbia = 6.17:1. Secondary text passes comfortably.
  - Inchiostro on carta = 17:1.
  - For reference, a darker orange such as #c2410c reaches 5.18:1 with white text and 4.91:1 on #fff8f2.

**Italy / culture**
- I found no peer-reviewed study on Italians' web color preferences or on the meaning of orange in Italy. The closest is a 2024 colour-taste expectation study comparing Italian, Polish and Moroccan young consumers. It concerns drinks, so it is not relevant to web trust. — [UNISS record](https://unifind.uniss.it/resource/item/284966). Popular claims such as "green = hope/luck in Italy" have no research basis ([Rosetta Stone blog](https://blog.rosettastone.com/?p=11837)).

### Inferences
- The warm cream plus single orange accent fits what the evidence supports: low complexity, a restrained palette and one high-salience color reserved for action. The risk is overusing orange. Isolation only works if orange is scarce. If orange fills large sections, badges, illustrations and the CTA alike, the CTA stops being the "isolated" item. Keep one accent color plus neutrals. Extra secondary accents (the file also defines miele #ffb23f and pesca #ffd3bc) should stay decorative and never compete with the CTA.
- A highly saturated orange signals energy and excitement (Labrecque & Milne; Gorn) more than competence or calm. For a studio selling to cautious small-business owners, lots of saturated orange can read as "promo/sale" energy. Balance it with large areas of calm neutral and dark text, which is roughly what the token set already does.
- Never use #ff5b22 for body text, links or thin outlines on cream (2.95:1). Use it as a fill with dark text, or for very large display text only. If a white-text orange button is wanted, darken the button fill to roughly #c2410c to #e04a12. This is a concrete, low-risk fix.
- Hue choice is not where conversion is won. No credible evidence says orange converts better than another color, and the button-color myth should not drive decisions.

### Gaps
- Exact optimal colorfulness/complexity levels and the Italy-specific data from Reinecke & Gajos 2014 (PDF not reachable). Worth checking the paper's per-country figures directly.
- No primary data on orange specifically, or on warm cream backgrounds and perceived warmth or trust.
- The widely cited HubSpot/Performable "red button +21%" test was not verified in this session. It is commonly used as evidence for hue, but it is better understood as a contrast effect.
- No Italian cultural color research relevant to web trust was found.

## Typography: readability, mobile size, line length, light/thin and wide display weights, secondary-text contrast, hierarchy

### Takeaway
Light and ultralight weights are measurably less legible and raise cognitive load, but the evidence concerns **body-size text**. At large display sizes, strokes are physically thicker and the penalty shrinks. So Archivo 300 at 40px and above is defensible, while weight 300 at small sizes or in light colors is not. Body text on this site (400 weight, 17–19px, 1.55 line-height, 6.8:1 secondary text) is in line with guidance. Wide (125%) display type has no direct legibility research. Its main costs are fewer words per line and more line breaks on mobile.

### Cited Findings
- Burmistrov et al. (NordiCHI 2016, eye tracking): light and ultra-light fonts were **less legible than regular and bold under both low and high contrast**, and induced higher cognitive load. The authors recommend avoiding light/ultralight for body text. — [unpaywall DOI 10.1145/2971485.2996745](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2971485.2996745); [PDF](https://flatisbad.com/publications/Burmistrov-NordiCHI16.pdf) (not fetchable here; summary from search results)
- A follow-up study on Helvetica Neue reported that users of all ages rated ultralight legibility very low, though younger users sometimes found it more aesthetically appealing. — [MSU conference record](https://istina.msu.ru/conferences/presentations/76363227/)
- Penn State Accessibility: light weights in dark gray on white rasterize partly to light gray and can fail contrast. "At larger sizes, the stroke is actually a little thicker, so the effects of rasterization are not as detrimental." — [Penn State: Beware light weight fonts](https://accessibility.psu.edu/2021/10/beware-light-weight-fonts)
- NN/g (Katie Sherwin), "Low-Contrast Text Is Not the Answer": low-contrast text may be trendy but is illegible, undiscoverable and inaccessible. — known only via secondary mention in search results; original at nngroup.com not fetchable. Treat the wording as unverified.
- Line length: Baymard recommends 50–75 characters per line for body text and says readability drops outside that range. — [Baymard: line length](https://baymard.com/blog/line-length-readability). Mobile guidance varies: European Commission component library says 40–60 characters ([EC typography](https://ec.europa.eu/component-library/ec/guidelines/typography)), other UX courses say 30–40 ([Uxcel](https://app.uxcel.com/lessons/mobile-layout-182/make-sure-the-text-is-readable-9583)).
- Mobile font size: Google's only hard threshold is that text under 12px is hard to read on mobile. Lighthouse flags pages where 40% or more of text is under 12px, so aim for at least 12px on at least 60% of text. — [web.dev font-size](https://web.dev/font-size); [Chrome Lighthouse docs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/lighthouse/seo/font-size). The common "16px minimum body text on mobile" is a convention (it also avoids iOS input zoom) found in UX courses, not a Google or Baymard research result ([Uxcel](https://app.uxcel.com/lessons/mobile-layout-182/make-sure-the-text-is-readable-9583)).
- Legibility research on letter width: widening narrow letters can help, and bolder strokes can improve legibility for low-vision readers, but optimal values are not well understood. — [PMC9804255](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9804255/); [RCA PhD thesis, von Ompteda](https://researchonline.rca.ac.uk/5084/). A glance-reading study found humanist styles more legible than square grotesques at a glance. — [PMC4612630](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4612630)
- Stanford credibility: "design look" was the most-cited credibility cue ([Consumer Reports](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility)). Typography is a large part of the "look," but there is no study isolating type.

### Inferences
- The site's current split is sound: weight 300 is used only for display and H2 (minimum computed size about 32–40px on mobile), while body is 400. The research penalty for light weights applies mainly at text sizes. Two risks to check: (1) any place where weight 300 at 125% width is used at small sizes (eyebrows, captions, buttons, mobile H2 near 32px) or on orange or nebbia backgrounds; (2) on low-DPI Windows screens, very thin strokes at mid sizes (32–48px) can still render faint. Moving the display weight from 300 to 350–400 would keep the elegant look with more stroke and costs nothing.
- Wide fonts (125%) at mega sizes such as clamp(3rem…10.5rem) produce very few words per line on a 375px phone. That means awkward breaks, hyphenation risk with long Italian words (for example "ristrutturazioni"), and headlines that push content below the fold. This is a layout and scanning cost, not a legibility one. No research found; this is design reasoning.
- Light, airy, wide display type tends to read as "editorial/premium/design studio." That is good for signaling craft to peers, but local-business owners may read it as less direct. This is an untested inference; A/B or 5-second tests with real prospects would settle it.
- Secondary text at 6.8:1 is good. Keep it at least 4.5:1 and do not lighten grafite for "elegance."
- Hierarchy: the site uses one family with width and weight variation, which keeps visual complexity low (consistent with Reinecke's finding that complexity drives appeal).

### Gaps
- No empirical study found on extended or wide display typefaces and perceived quality or trust.
- No research found linking font weight to perceived professionalism or warmth for service businesses.
- The NN/g low-contrast article and the Burmistrov PDF could not be fetched for exact numbers (reading speed and fixation deltas).

## Imagery: real photos vs illustrations vs stock, faces and gaze cues, founder photos, device mockups, illustration credibility

### Takeaway
The strongest, most replicated finding is NN/g's eye tracking: users ignore decorative and generic stock imagery but look at **real people and real products**, sometimes longer than at text. For a small service business, a real founder or team photo is the imagery with the best evidence for trust. Illustrations are not proven to lower credibility (one study found no significant difference), but people strongly prefer real photos. Gaze cues direct attention, and averted gaze toward the content beats eye contact for guiding attention. The phone mockup is "real product" imagery only if it shows real client work. There is no research on 3D or HTML device mockups specifically.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g, "Photos as Web Content" (Nielsen, 2010; now titled "Not All Images Are the Same"): "big feel-good images that are purely decorative" are ignored. Photos of products and real people, "as opposed to stock photos of models," are treated as content. On a FreshBooks team page, users spent 10% more time on real staff portraits than on bios, even though bios took 316% more space. Generic stock people were judged "pure filler." — [NN/g article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/) (via search summary; original not fetchable here); [UX Myths #8](https://uxmyths.com/post/705397950/myth-ornamental-graphics-improves-the-users-experience); [InfoToday 2010 report](https://www.infotoday.com/LinkUp/Being-Picture-Perfect-on-the-Web-72037.shtml). The study is from 2010 and based on a small number of eye-tracking sessions, but it is widely corroborated.
- BrightLocal consumer panel: 64% trusted a **real business owner photo** more than clip-art or a stock image of a plumber. — [BrightLocal research](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/images-inspire-trust-local-business-website/) (search summary; small panel, methodology only partly described, so treat it as directional). A "35% higher conversion with founder photo" claim circulates in vendor blogs without a named source ([Basch Solutions](https://www.baschsolutions.com/blog/stock-photos-are-killing-your-conversion-rate)). Unverified; do not cite.
- Fogg et al. (Stanford): an author photo raised trust and believability ratings of web content. — [meiert.com summary](https://meiert.com/en/blog/photos-make-websites-more-credible) (secondary; limited to one aspect of credibility)
- Newman (ANU): people are more likely to believe a claim, true or false, when it is paired with a photo, even a non-probative stock photo ("truthiness"). — [Australia Science TV](https://australiascience.tv/photos-make-people-believe). Photos raise belief independent of evidence.
- Photo vs illustration (Journal of Applied Communications, N = 492): a photo of a farmer vs AI-generated illustration vs cartoon versions showed **no statistically significant difference in credibility**. All were slightly positive, but preference was overwhelmingly for the photo (410 vs 43 illustration vs 39 cartoon). Respondents said photos felt "realistic and relatable." — [New Prairie Press](https://journals.newprairiepress.org/jac/article/id/20785/). Agricultural context, so applicability to web services is moderate.
- Aesthetic visuals give a credibility premium, but only when information is easy to process. — [CMMC Lab visual credibility project](https://sites.google.com/view/cmmclab/visual-credibility-project/are-visually-attractive-social-media-posts-more-credible)
- Gaze cues: in banner ads, a face with **averted gaze toward the product** increased attention to the product and improved brand/message memory compared with direct gaze. Mutual gaze pulled attention to the face itself. — [Sajjacholapunt & Ball 2014, PMC3941030](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3941030); [Frontiers in Psychology 2017, "Judgments at Gaze Value"](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00881/full) (gaze cuing guides attention and can shift purchase intention; animation captures attention on its own). Caveats: the literature is thin, and one cosmetics-ad study found that the eye-tracking data did not support the attention model, pointing to a social-signal explanation ([Univ. Vienna thesis](https://utheses-gateway.univie.ac.at/api/document/get/file/26825)).
- AI-generated imagery: NN/g tested 77 US adults on six versions of a fictional consulting firm's page that differed only in the hero image. They found **no evidence that the AI images reduced trust**; AI images even scored slightly higher on authenticity. Participants were not told about AI. — [NN/g: AI-generated images](https://nngroup.com/articles/ai-generated-images) (search summary; date not confirmed). Contrast: in a news context (N = 1,171, *Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly*), disclosed AI images reduced trust compared with real photos regardless of disclosure strategy ([SSRC MediaWell](https://mediawell.ssrc.org/citations/perceived-legitimacy-matters-building-public-trust-and-acceptance-of-ai-generated-news-images-through-strategic-ai-disclosure/)). A Clutch survey (Sept 2025) found 57% of consumers could not correctly spot AI photos despite 66% feeling confident ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250918110362/en/Clutch-Report-57-of-Consumers-Cant-Spot-AI-Photos-What-That-Means-for-Brands)). A small MTurk study (N = 199) found penalties only where craftsmanship and authenticity matter, such as apparel ([St. Mary's](https://commons.stmarytx.edu/msrjs/38)).
- Device mockups and 3D phones: **no research found** on whether device frames or 3D mockups increase trust or engagement.

### Inferences
- Replacing or supplementing illustrations with a **real photo of the founder or team** (named, local, ideally in a recognizable Italian setting) is the best-supported imagery change for a studio selling trust to small local businesses. Illustrations are not "wrong" (no significant credibility penalty was found), but they forfeit the attention and trust that real people get, and NN/g shows decorative images are ignored.
- Illustrations of the *client's* business are effectively decorative. Real screenshots of real client sites, or real before/after comparisons, are "product photos" in NN/g's sense and should draw attention.
- The HTML/3D phone mockup counts as product imagery if it shows real, identifiable client work, and it gets extra salience because animation captures attention (Frontiers 2017). If it shows invented demo businesses, it may be read as a template showcase. Label demos honestly.
- If a founder photo is used, have the person look toward the headline or CTA (averted gaze) rather than straight at the camera when the goal is directing attention. Use direct gaze where the goal is personal connection, such as an "about me" section.
- A fictional business presented as real, or AI-generated "team" photos, would carry a high trust risk if detected, especially for a service whose pitch is authenticity. The NN/g null result applies only when users don't know the images are AI.

### Gaps
- No study found specifically on illustrations vs photos for **web design/agency or service** sites.
- No research found on device mockups or 3D phone visuals and trust or conversion.
- BrightLocal sample size and date not confirmed (page not fetchable).
- NN/g AI-image article publication date not confirmed.

## The "generic AI-generated look" and template sameness: recognition and trust effects (2024–2026)

### Takeaway
There is broad practitioner agreement (2025–2026) that AI-built and template sites have converged on a recognizable look: purple/indigo gradients, Inter, three-icon card grids, centered hero, and similar. There is **no peer-reviewed evidence** yet that visitors recognize this look or that it lowers trust or conversion. Hard numbers circulating online, such as "82% share layouts" or "1.2% vs 2.8% conversion," are unsourced. The defensible argument goes through established research: first impressions are formed in milliseconds from visual cues; "design look" is the top credibility cue; and distinctiveness (von Restorff) aids noticing and memory. Sameness therefore plausibly hurts memorability, but it is not proven to hurt trust.

### Cited Findings
- Commentators describe "AI slop" design as tools defaulting to the statistically most common choices. Markers include purple gradients, the Inter font and three-box icon grids, described as "instantly recognizable." — [Noqta 2026](https://noqta.tn/en/blog/ai-design-slop-overused-ui-patterns-fix-2026); [prg.sh: "Why Your AI Keeps Building the Same Purple Gradient Website"](https://scour.ing/@emschwartz/p/https://prg.sh/ramblings/Why-Your-AI-Keeps-Building-the-Same-Purple-Gradient-Website); [Managed Code](https://managed-code.com/blog-post/why-ai-websites-look-the-same)
- Adam Wathan (Tailwind) reportedly apologized in August 2025 for making Tailwind UI buttons default to bg-indigo-500, which is cited as an origin of the indigo AI default (secondhand). — [prg.sh via scour.ing](https://scour.ing/@emschwartz/p/https://prg.sh/ramblings/Why-Your-AI-Keeps-Building-the-Same-Purple-Gradient-Website)
- Vendor argument: the costliest "built with AI" tell "isn't ugliness, it's sameness." A recognizable AI look is said to signal low effort and lead visitors to discount claims. — [Utsubo: 12 signals that drop trust in 2026](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/built-with-ai-trust-signals-2026); [Utsubo: AI vs custom design data](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/ai-website-vs-custom-design). These are vendor blogs with a commercial interest in custom design.
- Unverified stats: "82% of AI-generated sites share the same layouts," a 1.2% vs 2.8% conversion gap, and a "25% brand recall drop" appear in a single DEV post without sources. — [DEV Community](https://dev.to/steve_oak/vibe-coding-wont-make-your-product-stand-out-heres-what-will-4bj). **Do not cite as evidence.**
- Klaviyo/Datalily figure: 31% of consumers say visible AI-generated marketing content makes them trust a brand less (survey self-report). — cited by [Kuno Creative](https://www.kunocreative.com/blog/ai-brand-design-visual-differentiation)
- Counterpoint: NN/g found no trust penalty for undisclosed AI hero images (see Imagery). People are also poor at detecting AI imagery (Clutch: 57% wrong). — [NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/ai-generated-images); [BusinessWire/Clutch](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250918110362/en/Clutch-Report-57-of-Consumers-Cant-Spot-AI-Photos-What-That-Means-for-Brands)
- Jakob Nielsen (2025): image-generation quality is now high, and editorial selection is the hard part. — [UX Tigers](https://www.uxtigers.com/post/2025-images)
- Related robust research: Labrecque/CBS thesis found that adhering to category color norms gives more positive attitudes ([CBS](https://research.cbs.dk/en/studentProjects/competent-blue-and-exciting-red-examining-the-effect-of-logo-colo/)). Some typicality helps fluency, so "be different" is not universally better.

### Inferences
- For a design studio, the site *is* the portfolio. A templated or AI-default look damages the specific claim the studio sells ("we make you stand out"), even if general consumers don't consciously detect it. Target clients (small-business owners) compare studios, and sameness makes them hard to tell apart. This is reasoning, not measured.
- The current focale palette (cream plus mandarin orange, Archivo wide) avoids the documented AI tells (no purple gradient, no Inter, no three-icon grid), which is a positive. The parts most likely to read as "template" are generic illustrations and stock-like decoration. Real photography, real client work and specific local detail are the strongest de-templating moves, and they align with the NN/g evidence.
- Balance: keep conventions where users expect them (navigation, CTA placement, pricing layout). Distinctiveness should come from content (real people, real work, local specificity) and a coherent visual voice, not from breaking usability conventions.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study (2024–2026) found that measures whether visitors recognize "AI/template aesthetics" or how that affects trust or conversion.
- No Italian-market data on perceptions of templated or AI-looking business sites.
