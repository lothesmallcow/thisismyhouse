# Trust signals, social proof alternatives and conversion design for a new one-person web design studio (Italy, local SMB clients)

> Method note (Oct 2026): direct page fetches to nngroup.com, credibility.stanford.edu, istat.it and goodfirms.co were blocked by the sandbox network (DNS/proxy 403). Findings below come from web-search result extracts of those pages plus secondary summaries. Where a figure was only seen in a secondary source, it is flagged. The report writer should treat "[secondary]" items as weaker evidence.

## 1. Which credibility signals matter most for a service business (Stanford Web Credibility guidelines, NN/g trustworthiness factors)

### Takeaway
Two long-standing frameworks agree: visitors judge credibility first by visual design quality, then by evidence of a real, reachable organization/person behind the site, honest up-front disclosure (especially price), and fresh, complete content. For a brand-new studio with no clients, the site itself is the primary proof of competence, and "real person, real address, real contact, real prices" carries more weight than anything else it can show.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g's trustworthiness framework (Nielsen 1999, re-tested by NN/g in 2016) names four factors: design quality, up-front disclosure, comprehensive/correct/current content, and connection to the rest of the web. NN/g's 2016 study observed "these very same factors continue to influence users." — [NN/g: Trustworthiness in Web Design: 4 Credibility Factors](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/)
- Up-front disclosure: NN/g's example is showing extra charges (shipping) early rather than at checkout; hiding costs may win a few orders but drives away many more. — [NN/g: Trust or Bust](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/communicating-trustworthiness/) (seen via search extract)
- Connection to the rest of the web: linking out to third-party sources signals confidence because outside sources are more credible than self-description; isolated sites seem to be hiding something. — [NN/g: Trust or Bust](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/communicating-trustworthiness/) (seen via search extract)
- Stanford Web Credibility Guidelines (B.J. Fogg, Persuasive Technology Lab, 2002–2003) are ten guidelines. One central guideline: show there is a real organization behind the site, e.g. a street address, office photos, chamber of commerce membership; also highlight expertise/credentials and show trustworthy people (staff bios); avoid linking to non-credible sites (credibility by association). Other guidelines (widely reproduced) include: make it easy to verify accuracy, make contact easy, professional design appropriate to purpose, easy to use, update content often, restrained promotional content, avoid errors of all kinds. — [Stanford Web Credibility Project: Guidelines](http://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines/index.html); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Web_Credibility_Project). Note: guideline numbering differs between sources; text dates from 2002–2003.
- Stanford/Consumer WebWatch study (2002, 2,684 participants evaluating live sites): "design look" was the most frequently mentioned credibility factor, in 46.1% of comments; information structure/focus came next (~28.5%). Fogg: "To look good is to be good." Design comments were more common for finance (54.6%), search (52.6%), travel (50.5%), e-commerce (46.2%) sites. — [Consumer Reports Advocacy: How Do People Evaluate a Web Site's Credibility?](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility); [PDF](http://credibility.stanford.edu/pdf/How_Do_People_Evaluate_a_Web_Site's_Credibility_v37.pdf). Caveat: 46.1% is share of comments, not of participants (some press misreport it).
- Same research: experts (health/finance) gave more credibility to sites that disclose names and credentials of authors and cite reputable sources — i.e., experts weigh substance more than laypeople do. — [Consumer Reports Advocacy](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility)
- Stanford research found sites win credibility by clearly disclosing the people and the organization behind the site. — [Stanford guidelines](http://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines/index.html) (seen via search extract)
- Italian legal "up-front disclosure": art. 35 DPR 633/1972 requires the Partita IVA to appear on the homepage of a business website, including purely promotional sites (Agenzia delle Entrate risoluzione 60/E, 16 May 2006); fines reported as roughly €250–€2,000 (sources differ on exact amounts). — [Diritto.it](https://www.diritto.it/l-indicazione-della-partita-iva-sul-sito-web-e-obbligatoria/); [PMI.it](https://www.pmi.it/impresa/normativa/esperto/143885/sito-web-asd-regole.html); [FiscoeTasse](https://www.fiscoetasse.com/normativa-prassi/1004-partita-iva-sito-web.html)

### Inferences
- For a web design studio, "design quality" is doubly important: the site is both a credibility cue and a direct product sample. A prospect will reasonably infer "if their own site is clumsy, mine will be too."
- The Stanford "real organization" guideline maps directly onto a one-person studio: real name, face, city/area served, Partita IVA in the footer/homepage (legally required anyway), phone/WhatsApp, and optionally a Google Business Profile link — all verifiable, none faked.
- "Connection to the rest of the web" can be satisfied honestly by linking to the founder's LinkedIn, a Google Business Profile, a public GitHub/Dribbble, or recognized tools/standards (e.g., WCAG, Google PageSpeed results) rather than client logos.
- "Current content": a dated journal/notes section, a visible "last updated" or recent work date helps; an empty blog with one 2024 post hurts.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch the full 2016 NN/g article to quote its study details (participants, method) or which factor it found strongest.
- No study found that ranks the four NN/g factors specifically for local service businesses.

## 2. Social proof alternatives when you have none (founder visibility, guarantees/risk reversal, transparent pricing, process clarity, demo work, credentials, response time)

### Takeaway
Research supports several honest substitutes for testimonials: (a) visible real people, (b) price/cost transparency (increases trust and purchase interest in experiments), (c) showing process/effort ("operational transparency" raises perceived value), (d) guarantees that reduce perceived risk especially for hard-to-judge "experience" services, and (e) fast, promised response times. Evidence strength varies; none of it is specific to web design studios.

### Cited Findings
**Founder visibility / real people**
- NN/g About-Us research: participants liked photos of real employees and products; realistic photography helped organizations appear authentic and transparent. — [NN/g: 'About Us' Information on Websites](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-information-on-websites/)
- NN/g eyetracking: users spent more time on portrait photos than on the accompanying biographies; showing real people helps users associate real humans with an otherwise faceless company. The benefit applies to real people, not models. — [NN/g: Photos as Web Content](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)
- NN/g homepage guideline: use photos of identifiable people connected to the content, not models or generic stock. — [NN/g: 113 Homepage Guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/113-design-guidelines-homepage-usability/)
- [secondary, unverified] A Webmedic article attributes to Baymard a finding that team photos with real names raise perceived trustworthiness; attribution could not be verified. — [Webmedic](https://webmedic.com/ecommerce-about-page)

**Price / cost transparency**
- Mohan, Buell & John, "Lifting the Veil: The Benefits of Cost Transparency" (Marketing Science 2020; HBS WP 15-017): one field study + six lab experiments; voluntarily disclosing unit costs increased purchase interest, mediated by trust ("an act of intimate disclosure"); INFORMS press release reports up to ~20% sales increase; effect only when disclosure is voluntary. — [HBS PDF](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/15-017_f67df7f5-8336-47fc-a0c1-098838aa550d.pdf); [INFORMS press release](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/New-Research-Finds-Cost-Transparency-Can-Increase-Sales-20)
- NN/g: pricing is the top information need reported by business customers; when sites hid prices, study participants went to competitors; "contact us for a quote" should not be assumed to work in early research; hiding information makes the organization seem dubious; prices act as a lead filter. For variable/custom services, show starting prices, ranges, or prices for common scenarios/sample packages. — [NN/g: Pricing information gives B2B sites a competitive advantage](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/); [NN/g: Show Prices for Common Scenarios](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-prices-for-common-scenarios/). Caveat: NN/g articles date 2006–2016.

**Process clarity / operational transparency**
- Buell & Norton, "The Labor Illusion" (Management Science 2011), five experiments: showing the work being done raised perceived value; mediated by reciprocity; participants sometimes preferred the visibly effortful option even with identical results. Effect ~8% higher perceived value in the travel-site experiment [secondary: Marketing Week]. — [INFORMS abstract](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376); [Marketing Week (Shotton)](https://www.marketingweek.com/richard-shotton-labour-illusion/)
- Caveat: transparency amplifies existing impressions; in the dating context it made users unhappier after a poor match. — [Agile Brand Guide summary](https://agilebrandguide.com/wiki/terms/labor-illusion/) [secondary]
- Buell, Kim & Tsay (2017), "Creating Reciprocal Value Through Operational Transparency": showing customers the process raised perceived quality. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2449029)
- Buell, Porter & Norton (government services): operational transparency increased trust and engagement; "none of our studies demonstrated that transparency undermined trust." — [HBS working paper](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Buell_Porter_Norton_01262020_WP_1c84113c-821c-4443-bfb6-3679722343a1.pdf)

**Guarantees / risk reversal**
- Money-back guarantees (MBGs) significantly reduce perceived performance and financial risk, especially for experience goods (quality hard to judge before purchase); stricter conditions can make a guarantee more believable for experience goods. — [Journal of Retailing via ScienceDirect: Understanding Money-Back Guarantees](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022435911000820); [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/18596091/Understanding_Money_Back_Guarantees_Cognitive_Affective_and_Behavioral_Outcomes)
- Signaling theory (Moorthy & Srinivasan 1995): when quality is hard to judge beforehand, a guarantee can raise perceived quality and purchase intentions. — [ScienceDirect: retailer MBGs](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0148296313003603)
- Online luxury study (210 respondents, SEM): MBG reduced perceived risk and raised purchase intention; MBG was a stronger signal than a quality label or website quality. Weak (convenience sample). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329339443_Buying_Luxury_Brands_Online_The_effect_of_money-back_guarantee_on_perceived_risk_and_purchase_intention)
- Guarantee believability depends on generosity, store-name familiarity and price — i.e., an unknown seller's very generous guarantee may be discounted. — [ResearchGate: Do Consumers Perceive MBGs as Believable?](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286120810_Do_Consumers_Perceive_Money-Back_Guarantees_as_Believable_The_Effects_of_Money-Back_Guarantee_Generosity_Store_Name_Familiarity_and_Perceived_Price)

**Response time**
- HBR audit (2011) of 2,241 US companies sending a web lead: 37% replied within an hour, 23% never replied; average responder reply time 42 hours. Companion analysis of 1.25M leads: firms contacting within an hour were ~7x as likely to qualify the lead as those waiting one hour longer, and >60x vs those waiting 24h+. [secondary reproductions; original HBR article "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads" not fetched] — [ainora.lt compilation](https://ainora.lt/blog/lead-response-time-statistics-every-study-2026); [Venture Harbour](https://ventureharbour.com/how-fast-do-marketing-leads-turn-cold-and-how-to-stop-it-happening/)
- Caution: the "100x / 21x" speed-to-lead figures often credited to Harvard come from a 2007 MIT/InsideSales study. — [ainora.lt](https://ainora.lt/blog/lead-response-time-statistics-every-study-2026)

**Ethics / legal constraint on fake proof**
- EU Omnibus Directive 2019/2161 bans publishing, commissioning or manipulating fake consumer reviews; Italy transposed it via D.Lgs. 26/2023 (Codice del Consumo arts. 22–23), which also requires disclosing whether/how reviews are verified as coming from real customers. — [NT+ Diritto (Il Sole 24 Ore)](https://ntplusdiritto.ilsole24ore.com/art/la-direttiva-omnibus-nuove-regole-poste-tutela-consumatori-AEsGCvGD); [Agenda Digitale](https://www.agendadigitale.eu/mercati-digitali/recensioni-certificate-cosa-puo-fare-un-ecommerce-per-tutelarsi-ed-essere-a-norma/)
- In Italy, 8.6% of reviews published in 2024 were fake (TripAdvisor data) and 66% of consumers say they regularly encounter unreliable reviews — i.e., buyers are primed to distrust suspicious proof. — [AGI, June 2025](https://www.agi.it/economia/news/2025-06-15/recensioni-online-impatto-falsita-proposta-normativa-31875921/)

### Inferences
- Honest substitute stack for a zero-client studio, ordered by evidence strength: (1) visible founder (real photo, name, area, P.IVA, direct phone/WhatsApp); (2) published prices or "from €X" packages with what's included and what's extra; (3) a clear step-by-step process with timelines and deliverables (labor-illusion/operational transparency); (4) a risk-reversal offer (free preview/mockup of their homepage, pay only on approval, or staged payments) worded with clear, specific conditions so it is believable from an unknown seller; (5) a concrete response-time promise ("rispondo entro X ore lavorative") that the founder can actually keep.
- A "free preview, pay on approval" offer is a strong fit for an experience-good service, but (per AIGA, see section 3) it is spec work; it is ethically fine when it is the founder's own chosen sales model and scoped (e.g., homepage mockup only), but should not be framed as a contest.
- Showing the work (before/after of a demo, process snapshots, annotated decisions) leverages operational transparency honestly without implying clients exist.
- Because guarantee credibility drops for unknown sellers making generous claims, guarantees should be narrow, specific and plausible ("if you don't like the preview, you pay nothing") rather than sweeping ("100% satisfaction or double your money back").
- Although Omnibus targets consumer (B2C) practices, inventing testimonials/logos for B2B clients would plausibly also fall under misleading advertising rules; this was not verified (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No experimental evidence found specifically on guarantees or "pay on approval" for web design/creative services; service-guarantee literature (Hart; hotel experiments) was referenced but not retrieved.
- No study found that directly measures the conversion lift of published prices for small creative agencies (NN/g evidence is B2B usability, qualitative).
- Did not verify whether Italian misleading-advertising law for B2B (D.Lgs. 145/2007) explicitly covers fake testimonials; inference only.

## 3. Portfolio and case study presentation (what prospects look for, NN/g on portfolios, spec/demo work and honest labeling)

### Takeaway
Reviewers scan rather than read; they want a clear problem, the designer's role, reasoning, and outcome/impact, ideally from a similar industry. A new studio can show demo/concept projects legitimately if they are clearly labeled as concepts (not client work), and present them as case studies of reasoning (problem diagnosis → decisions → result), not invented metrics.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g survey of 204 UX hiring managers: portfolios should be scannable; reviewers "very rarely" read an entire portfolio word for word. Recommended: 3–5 detailed case studies, each starting from the problem or hypothesis, the designer's specific role, challenges and discarded ideas. — [NN/g: 5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/); [NN/g video: UX Portfolios – What Hiring Managers Look For](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/ux-portfolios-hiring/)
- NN/g (researcher portfolios): reviewers are making a business decision so they want impact; concentrate case studies on impact. — [NN/g: Portfolios for UX Researchers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-researcher-portfolio/)
- NN/g example of an agency case study (Marketade) moving from qualitative research to measurable results. — [NN/g: Quantifying UX Improvements: A Case Study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quantifying-case-study/)
- Practitioner guidance (lower authority): the outcome section answers the client's real question, "did this person actually move the needle?"; "process demonstrates judgment; a polished final result demonstrates execution." — [mnml: Portfolio case study template](https://mnml.page/blog/portfolio-case-study-template)
- Agency-selection guides (opinion, not survey): clients look for relevant industry experience, testimonials mentioning professionalism/responsiveness/deadlines; red flags include cookie-cutter templates, no mobile examples, no metrics or case studies, sites that all look alike. — [FasterCapital](https://fastercapital.com/topics/assessing-agency-portfolio-and-case-studies.html); [Utsubo 2026 guide](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/choose-web-design-agency-guide)
- AIGA position: designers should enter projects with full engagement; speculative work = work done before engagement in anticipation of payment; AIGA strongly encourages awareness of risks but (since its updated stance) recognizes the decision is up to individual designers. AIGA Standards of Professional Practice 4.2 discourages speculative projects where pay depends on acceptance, including preliminary schematic proposals. — [AIGA Position on Spec Work (2024 PDF)](https://www.aiga.org/sites/default/files/2024-07/2024-AIGA-Position-on-Spec-Work.pdf); [ICoD news on AIGA position](https://www.theicod.org/resources/news-archive/aiga-maintains-its-position-against-speculative-work-while-recognising-that-the-decision-is-up-to-individual-designers); [Catapult quoting AIGA 4.2](https://catapultu.com/spec-work/)
- AIGA 2026 portfolio checklist advises starting with the work and a clear brief and showing role boundaries; it does not explicitly cover labeling concept work. — [AIGA Design Careers checklist 2026](https://designcareers.aiga.org/career-resources/portfolio-13/graphic-design-portfolio-review-checklist-2026-137)
- Where client work is confidential, NN/g suggests asking past clients for testimonials instead. — [NN/g: UX design portfolios](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)

### Inferences
- For a new studio targeting renovation firms, window fitters, B&Bs, agriturismi and wedding venues, similar-industry demos are the single most persuasive portfolio element available: prospects pattern-match ("that's a site for someone like me").
- Honest labeling pattern: a visible tag such as "Progetto dimostrativo" / "Concept — attività di fantasia" on cards and case pages, a one-line explanation of why concepts exist ("Studio nuovo: questi progetti mostrano come lavoro"), no fake business names that could be mistaken for real local firms, no invented KPIs. Results can be framed as measurable properties of the build itself (Lighthouse/PageSpeed scores, page weight, load time, accessibility checks), which are true and verifiable.
- An unsolicited redesign of a real business's site shown publicly risks implying a relationship and could misuse their brand; if used, label "redesign non richiesto, a titolo dimostrativo" and avoid their logo/trademarks, or anonymize. (Inference; no legal source retrieved.)
- Structure each case page for scanning: problem (what's wrong with a typical site in that sector) → what I changed and why → before/after → measurable build facts → CTA.

### Gaps
- No NN/g article found specifically on agency/studio portfolios from the client's (buyer's) perspective; NN/g evidence is from hiring-manager research and is transferred by analogy.
- No Clutch or GoodFirms survey data retrieved on buyer selection criteria for web design agencies (GoodFirms' small-business web design research surveyed designers, not buyers; page could not be fetched). — [GoodFirms](https://www.goodfirms.co/blog/web-design-small-businesses-research-findings)
- No source found with explicit, authoritative guidance on how to label spec/concept work in a portfolio.

## 4. CTA and form design (number of CTAs, primary vs secondary, CTA copy, form fields, WhatsApp/phone in Italy, friction)

### Takeaway
Fewer competing actions and fewer, simpler fields convert better; a phone field without explanation is a known friction point. One primary action repeated down the page plus a lower-commitment secondary path (WhatsApp/phone) fits Italian behavior, where WhatsApp is near-universal. Button-copy "hacks" (my vs your) are page-specific and weakly evidenced.

### Cited Findings
**Number of CTAs**
- Unbounce analysis of 18,639 landing pages [secondary; dataset ~2018]: pages with a single link/CTA averaged 13.5% conversion vs 11.9% for 2–3 and 10.5% for more. The widely repeated "266% lift" for a single CTA appears only in secondary summaries. Repeating the same goal down a long page is not the same as competing CTAs. — [MarketingProfs infographic](https://www.marketingprofs.com/charts/2018/33935/what-converts-web-landing-page-benchmarks-and-trends-infographic); [Colorlib stats](https://colorlib.com/wp/landing-page-statistics/)
- Reading level [secondary, inconsistent]: Unbounce Q4 2024 data reportedly show 5th–7th grade copy converting at 11.1% vs 5.3% for professional/college-level copy. — [Shno landing page statistics](https://www.shno.co/marketing-statistics/landing-page-conversion-statistics)

**CTA copy**
- [secondary, weak] Michael Aagaard (ContentVerve/Unbounce) reported ~90% click lift changing "your" to "my" on one page, but a 24.95% drop on a payment page; page-specific. A study of 250+ CTA copy tests found rewording a button wins about one in eight times. — [Review42](https://resources.review42.com/call-to-action-tips/); [Marketing Monk](https://marketingmonk.so/tools/cta-generator)

**Form length and fields**
- HubSpot (Dan Zarrella) analysis of 40,000+ landing pages: single-line text fields were not tied to major drop-off, but text areas and drop-down selects were; asking age and phone lowered conversions. Secondary reconstructions report ~25% conversion with 3 fields vs ~21% with 5 [figures inferred from graphs; treat as approximate]. — [HubSpot: Which Types of Form Fields Lower Conversions?](https://blog.hubspot.com/blog/tabid/6307/bid/6746/which-types-of-form-fields-lower-landing-page-conversions.aspx); [HubSpot: 3 Form Fields That Kill Conversion](https://blog.hubspot.com/blog/tabid/6307/bid/6748/3-form-fields-that-kill-landing-page-conversion-rates.aspx)
- Baymard (2024): average checkout has 11.3 form fields (down from 12.8 in 2019); most sites need only ~8; number of fields impacts usability more than number of steps. — [Baymard: Checkout flow average form fields](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields)
- Baymard: users hesitate to give phone numbers (fear of marketing calls, identity theft); in testing, some gave false data or abandoned when a phone field was required without explanation; 39% of benchmarked sites required phone without explanation. Fix: explain why, make optional, or remove. — [Baymard: Explain the phone number field](https://baymard.com/blog/explain-phone-number-field)
- Zuko form analytics: phone number has one of the highest abandonment rates among common fields (behind password). — [Zuko](https://www.zuko.io/blog/optimizing-the-phone-number-field-on-forms)
- [weak] A claimed "2024 HubSpot study: 4.1% drop per added field" could not be verified. — [Ivristech](https://ivristech.com/form-conversion-rate-benchmarks/)

**WhatsApp / phone in Italy**
- We Are Social/Meltwater Digital 2026: WhatsApp is used by 90.1% of Italian internet users aged 16+ (Digital 2025 estimated ~31 million regular users; different metrics). — [Agenda Digitale](https://www.agendadigitale.eu/mercati-digitali/whatsapp-nelle-aziende-italiane-come-cambia-il-rapporto-coi-clienti/)
- Osservatorio WhatsApp Marketing Italia 2026 (via Agenda Digitale; primary not found): 73% of surveyed companies already use WhatsApp with customers/prospects; 58.5% use the plain WhatsApp Business App; ~81% use it mainly for customer care. — [Agenda Digitale](https://www.agendadigitale.eu/mercati-digitali/whatsapp-nelle-aziende-italiane-come-cambia-il-rapporto-coi-clienti/)
- Esendex (vendor research, treat cautiously): 83% of Italian respondents willing to answer a WhatsApp message from a company. — [01net](https://www.01net.it/whatsapp-business-guidera-mobile-messaging-nel-il-2025-secondo-esendex/)
- Coverage notes high customer demand but slow/immature business adoption of WhatsApp in Italy. — [CMI Magazine](https://www.cmimagazine.it/30976-whatsapp-per-le-aziende-italiane-alta-domanda-adozione-ancora-lenta/); [Agenda Digitale: il nodo è la maturità digitale](https://www.agendadigitale.eu/mercati-digitali/whatsapp-nelle-imprese-italiane-il-nodo-e-la-maturita-digitale/)

### Inferences
- Recommended pattern: one primary CTA goal (e.g., "Richiedi l'anteprima gratuita del tuo sito" / "Chiedi un preventivo") repeated at natural decision points; one secondary low-friction channel (WhatsApp click-to-chat with prefilled message, and tap-to-call) shown consistently; avoid adding newsletter, social follows, etc. as competing actions.
- Form: 3–4 fields (name, business/current site URL, contact preference + contact, optional message). Avoid drop-downs/large required text areas; if phone is asked, make it optional or explain ("per richiamarti o scriverti su WhatsApp, niente spam").
- CTA copy should name the concrete, low-risk outcome and remove uncertainty (what happens next, how fast, cost: "gratis, senza impegno"), rather than relying on pronoun tests.
- Because Italian owners of renovation firms and B&Bs commonly run business on WhatsApp, offering WhatsApp as an equal contact path likely reduces friction more than any form optimization — but this is inferred from usage data, not from a conversion experiment.

### Gaps
- No primary CXL data retrieved on form fields or CTAs.
- No Italian conversion experiment found comparing WhatsApp vs form vs phone for lead generation.
- Unbounce primary reports not accessible; magnitudes are from secondary sources.

## 5. Buyer psychology of small (Italian) business owners choosing a web designer

### Takeaway
Owner-side survey data on hiring web designers is scarce; practitioner sources consistently cite fear of hidden/ongoing costs, losing ownership/control (domain, access), and non-responsiveness. Italian SMEs rely more than the EU average on personal relationships and word of mouth when choosing suppliers, and local consumers lean heavily on online reviews — so a new studio must substitute personal accessibility and transparency for missing reputation.

### Cited Findings
- No survey of small business owners on hiring web designers was found; GoodFirms' small-business web design research surveyed 200+ agencies/freelancers, not buyers. — [GoodFirms](https://www.goodfirms.co/blog/web-design-small-businesses-research-findings)
- Practitioner blogs: hidden costs beneath the quoted price are "one of the most common complaints"; designer registering the domain in their own name leaves owner without leverage; no discussion of ownership/file access is a red flag. — [Build 76 (UK agency)](https://www.build76.co.uk/blog/hidden-costs-of-hiring-a-web-designer) [opinion]
- Vendor estimate: budget at least $500–$1,000/year for ongoing website costs (domain, hosting, SSL, maintenance), which owners commonly underestimate. — [Richwood Marketing](https://richwoodmarketing.com/blog/website-design-cost-small-business/) [vendor, weak]
- HBR audit: 23% of companies never responded to a web lead (relevant to the "ghosting" fear from the buyer side; the buyer has likely experienced unresponsive vendors). — [Venture Harbour summary](https://ventureharbour.com/how-fast-do-marketing-leads-turn-cold-and-how-to-stop-it-happening/) [secondary]
- Geopost Business Barometer (Oct 2026): Italian SMEs use sales representatives (45% vs EU 35%) and word of mouth (35% vs EU 27%) more than the EU average when sourcing suppliers; 18% of Italian SMEs use AI tools to search for suppliers (EU 15%). — [Il Giornale della Logistica](https://ilgiornaledellalogistica.it/trend/geopost-business-barometer-pmi/); [La Mescolanza](https://www.lamescolanza.com/2026/10/07/pmi-lai-entra-negli-acquisti-in-italia-la-consegna-conta-piu-del-prezzo/). Note: logistics/supply context, not web services.
- [secondary, weak] An article citing the Politecnico di Milano Osservatorio Innovazione Digitale nelle PMI 2025–26 claims word of mouth outweighs any credential when Italian SMEs choose marketing suppliers; primary not verified. — [Diario Innovazione](https://diarioinnovazione.it/prossimita-o-specializzazione-come-si-sceglie-chi-affidare-marketing-propria-azienda); [Blurr](https://blurr.it/come-pmi-italiane-scelgono-web-agency/)

Exact website share could not be extracted. — [Istat comunicato](https://www.istat.it/comunicato-stampa/imprese-e-ict-anno-2025/); [Istat Statreport PDF](https://www.istat.it/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Statreport_ICT2025.pdf); [Key4biz](https://www.key4biz.it/ai-raddoppia-luso-nelle-imprese-italiane-dall82-del-2024-al-164-del-2025-i-dati-istat/559811/)

### Inferences
- Main fears to defuse explicitly on the site, in plain Italian without jargon: (1) hidden or recurring costs → state total price, what's included, yearly running costs; (2) loss of control → "dominio e sito intestati a te, accessi consegnati"; (3) ghosting → named person, direct number, response-time promise, written timeline; (4) being sold something they don't need → small fixed-scope packages.
- In a word-of-mouth culture, a new studio can make itself "referable": easy-to-forward WhatsApp link, a clear one-line description of the offer, local area named, and a willingness to meet in person/call — proximity is a trust proxy for local Italian owners (inference; supported only indirectly by Geopost supplier data).
- Because the clients' own customers (guests, couples, homeowners) rely heavily on reviews, demos can show how the redesigned site integrates genuine Google/Booking reviews — a selling point that is about the client's proof, not fabricated studio proof.

### Gaps
- No Italian or international survey found directly measuring small-business owners' fears/decision criteria when hiring a web designer; Clutch/GoodFirms buyer surveys not retrieved.
- Politecnico di Milano Osservatorio primary data not accessed; word-of-mouth claim for marketing suppliers is unverified.
- Istat 2025 share of small enterprises (10–49 employees) with a website not extracted (micro-enterprises under 10 employees — the actual target — are generally outside Istat ICT survey scope; not verified).

## 6. Personal vs company framing ("we" vs a named person with a face)

### Takeaway
No controlled research was found on "I" vs "we" for one-person studios. NN/g evidence on real-people photos plus Stanford's "show the real people behind the site" favor a named, visible founder; practitioner opinion is split, with the main risk of "we" being perceived dishonesty when the client discovers it's one person.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g: real portraits draw more attention than bios and humanize a faceless organization; real employees preferred to stock models. — [NN/g: Photos as Web Content](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/); [NN/g About Us research](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-information-on-websites/)
- Stanford: credibility rises when the site discloses the people and organization behind it; show trustworthy people (bios). — [Stanford guidelines](http://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines/index.html)
- Practitioner opinion for "I": first-person copy makes clients know and trust the freelancer sooner and is more authentic for a one-person business. — [Bigmouth Copy](https://bigmouthcopy.com/blog/plural-vs-singular-pronouns)
- Practitioner opinion: "I" is a selling point since many prospects want to deal with an individual, not a project manager; "we" can look bigger/more dependable to corporate clients. — [Creative Boost](https://creative-boost.com/i-or-we/)
- "We" can be justified when the solo operator uses collaborators (photographers, copywriters). — [Millo](https://millo.co/we-vs-i-the-best-pronoun-for-a-freelance-business)
- Forum participants: a solo business claiming to be a team can come across as dishonest once discovered; middle path is "we" for the business's deliverables and "I" for personal commitments. — [UK Business Forums](https://www.ukbusinessforums.co.uk/threads/1-person-companies-we-or-i.397918/)

### Inferences
- For small local Italian owners (who value personal relationships and fear being passed to anonymous juniors or ghosted), a named founder with a face and direct contact is likely the stronger frame; "we" signals scale these buyers don't need and risks a credibility hit at first call.
- Acceptable hybrid: studio brand name for the business + first-person singular in About, process and promises ("ti rispondo io", "lavoro io sul tuo sito"); "noi" only where true (e.g., with named collaborators). If the site currently uses "noi" voice, the About section should still make clear it is one person.

### Gaps
- No A/B test or academic study found comparing "I" vs "we" framing for freelancers/solo studios; all evidence is practitioner opinion (mostly 3–5 years old).
- No Italian-specific evidence on pronoun/persona framing.
