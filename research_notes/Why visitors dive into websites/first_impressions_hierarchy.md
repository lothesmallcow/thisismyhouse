# First impressions, above-the-fold structure, focal points and visual hierarchy

Research note, October 2026. Access caveat: in this environment, direct page fetches to nngroup.com, research.google, credibility.stanford.edu and cxl.com failed (DNS errors). The findings below come from search-engine extractions of those pages and from abstracts and secondary summaries. Primary URLs are cited wherever they were identified. Numbers that only appeared in secondary summaries are flagged as such.

## 1. What the first-impression studies say (Lindgaard 2006, Tuch/Google 2012, Reinecke 2013/2014) and what predicts a good first impression

### Takeaway
People judge a site's visual appeal in 50 ms or less, and those judgments are very stable. At 500 ms they closely match the 50 ms ratings, and they also color judgments of trust and usability. The best-supported predictors of a good first impression are low visual complexity and high prototypicality: the page looks like what people expect that kind of site to look like. Colorfulness matters too, but the preferred amount varies with age, gender and country.

### Cited Findings
**Lindgaard et al. (2006)**
- Citation: Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek and Brown (2006), "Attention web designers: You have 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression!", *Behaviour & Information Technology* 25(2), 115–126, doi:10.1080/01449290500330448 — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Attention-web-designers:-You-have-50-milliseconds-a-Lindgaard-Fernandes/f9715b117c57d4e7064afe1c1cb95d5bf4cc1831); [Making Good citation](https://www.makinggood.ac.nz/l/lindgaard,-g,-fernandes,-g,-dudek,-c,-brown,-j-(2006)-attention-web-designers-you-have-50-milliseconds-to-make-a-good-first-impression!-behaviour-it,-25,-115%E2%80%93126/)
- The paper had three studies. Studies 1–2 showed homepages for 500 ms. Study 3 added a 50 ms condition to test whether the effect was a "mere exposure effect." The abstract says visual appeal ratings were highly correlated from one phase to the next, and between the 50 ms and 500 ms conditions. Its conclusion is that "visual appeal can be assessed within 50 ms" — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Attention-web-designers:-You-have-50-milliseconds-a-Lindgaard-Fernandes/f9715b117c57d4e7064afe1c1cb95d5bf4cc1831)
- Study 2 found visual appeal closely related to most of seven specific design dimensions — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Attention-web-designers:-You-have-50-milliseconds-a-Lindgaard-Fernandes/f9715b117c57d4e7064afe1c1cb95d5bf4cc1831)

**Lindgaard et al. (2011), the follow-up**
- Lindgaard, Dudek, Sen, Sumegi and Noonan, ACM TOCHI 18(1). Participants saw homepages for 50 ms, then judged appeal, trustworthiness or perceived usability. The abstract says "all three types of judgments are largely driven by visual appeal," though the more cognitively demanding judgments rely on somewhat different visual attributes — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1959022.1959023); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/An-exploration-of-relations-between-visual-appeal,-Lindgaard-Dudek/5c9a148d8651d98a664b5c5f00d3afd1c6564790)

**Tuch et al. (2012), Google / University of Basel**
- Citation: Tuch, Presslaber, Stöcklin, Opwis and Bargas-Avila (2012), "The role of visual complexity and prototypicality regarding first impression of websites", *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies* 70(11), 794–811 — [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/); [PDF](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/38315.pdf); [edoc Basel](https://edoc.unibas.ch/21676)
- Study 1 used 119 real website screenshots, varied in visual complexity (VC) and prototypicality (PT). Presentation time (50 / 500 / 1000 ms) was a between-subject factor. VC and PT affected aesthetics ratings within the first 50 ms — [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/)
- Study 2 cut exposure to 17, 33 and 50 ms. Both VC and PT still affected ratings at 17 ms, but PT's effect was weaker at the shortest exposures, so complexity is processed before prototypicality. Overall, websites with low VC and high PT were rated as highly appealing — [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/)
- Google's research blog (August 2012) summarized this as "Users love simple and familiar designs." In the authors' terms, "familiar" means prototypical for the site's category — [Google Research Blog](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/); [mirror](https://ai.googleblog.com/2012/08/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs.html)

**Reinecke et al. (2013), CHI**
- Reinecke, Yeh, Miratrix, Mardiko, Zhao, Liu and Gajos (2013), "Predicting users' first impressions of website aesthetics with a quantification of perceived visual complexity and colorfulness", CHI 2013, 2049–2058, doi:10.1145/2470654.2481281. It built computational models of perceived colorfulness and visual complexity from screenshots — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-cc85-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Predicting-users'-first-impressions-of-website-with-Reinecke-Yeh/aa6baf340d80f61d3e4a770d107b20d7e26878a7)
- Combined with demographics (education, age), the models explained about half of the variance in appeal ratings given after 500 ms of viewing. This figure comes from a summary of the paper and was not checked against the full text — [EdLab, Columbia](https://edlab.tc.columbia.edu/blog/9085-First-Impression-Counts-Predicting-Website-Aesthetics-is-Possible)
- Participant counts differ by source: the CHI 2013 program says 548 volunteers, and an earlier EdLab preview says 242 — [CHI 2013 program](http://chi2013.acm.org/program/by-day/wednesday/); [EdLab](https://edlab.tc.columbia.edu/blog/9085-First-Impression-Counts-Predicting-Website-Aesthetics-is-Possible)

**Reinecke and Gajos (2014), CHI: "Quantifying visual preferences around the world"**
- Paper: doi:10.1145/2556288.2557052 — [Harvard paper page](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2014/reinecke14visual.shtml)
- According to a popular-science summary, people over 40 preferred more colorful sites, and the preference was stronger over 50. Women preferred more colorful sites than men. People in Finland, Russia and Poland preferred less color, people in Malaysia, Chile and Macedonia preferred more, and neighboring countries had similar preferences. These details are secondary and not verified against the paper — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/sg/blog/brain-wise/201510/age-gender-geography-and-visual-appeal-0)

**Baughan, August, Yamashita and Reinecke (2020), CHI: "Keep it Simple"**
- This study had 165 participants. Visual complexity had a significant negative effect on both search efficiency and information recall. More complex pages took longer to find information on. Participants who preferred simple sites were hurt more by complex pages — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3313831.3376849?cid=99659527940); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/341696258_Keep_it_Simple_How_Visual_Complexity_and_Preferences_Impact_Search_Efficiency_on_Websites)
- A CHI 2021 follow-up found Japanese participants took significantly longer than US participants to find information, and the gap grew as complexity increased — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3411764.3445519); [PDF](https://wildlab.cs.washington.edu/Publications_files/Baughan_Oliveira_CHI2021.pdf)

### Inferences
- For the studio's homepage, the first 50–500 ms are decided by gestalt: overall complexity, color and how much it looks like the kind of site visitors expect. Copy has no effect in that window. A busy hero (several competing animations, a device mockup, notification toasts, badges and decorative shapes all visible at once) raises perceived complexity, which is the factor processed first.
- "Prototypicality" means a studio site should still look like a studio or agency site: a clear nav, a headline, proof of work and a contact route. Novel structures, such as slide-like full-screen scenes without a recognizable website skeleton, may lower prototypicality and therefore first-impression appeal. The studio's previous task list mentions moving the home from "slides" back toward "a website," which is consistent with this.
- The audience is Italian small-business owners, many of them likely over 40. The Reinecke 2014 summary suggests this group tolerates or prefers somewhat more color, but the complexity penalty still applies. Warm color is fine, clutter is not. No Italy-specific data was found.
- First-impression appeal spills over into trust and perceived usability (Lindgaard 2011). For a studio selling design, the homepage's own visual quality works as the product demo.

### Gaps
- No effect sizes (eta-squared, exact correlation coefficients) could be extracted for Lindgaard 2006 or Tuch 2012 because the full texts could not be fetched. The often-quoted r ≈ .9x correlation between 50 ms and 500 ms ratings is not verified here.
- Italy's specific position in the Reinecke 2014 country-preference data was not found.
- There is no first-impression research specific to mobile in this set. All of these studies used desktop screenshots.

## 2. How long users stay before deciding, and what must be visible above the fold (NN/g on dwell time, the fold, scrolling)

### Takeaway
Users often leave a page within 10–20 seconds. The leave-probability is highest early, so a page that communicates its value proposition in about 10 seconds earns minutes of attention. Attention drops sharply at the fold. In NN/g's 2018 eyetracking, 57% of viewing time was above the fold and 74% in the first two screenfuls, down from 80% above the fold in 2010. People do scroll, but only when what is above the fold promises enough.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g, "How Long Do Users Stay on Web Pages?" (Nielsen, 2011). Users often leave web pages in 10–20 seconds, but pages with a clear value proposition can hold attention much longer. "To gain several minutes of user attention, you must clearly communicate your value proposition within 10 seconds." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- That article draws on Microsoft Research (Chao Liu et al.), who analyzed visit durations for 205,873 pages and found dwell time follows a Weibull distribution with "negative aging." The chance of leaving is highest in the first seconds and falls the longer a user stays. The interpretation of "negative aging" is standard Weibull reading, not quoted text — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- NN/g, "Scrolling and Attention" (2018 update). The study recorded 130,000+ fixations from 120 participants on a 1920×1080 screen. About 57% of page-viewing time was above the fold and 74% in the first two screenfuls (up to 2160 px). The 2010 figure was 80% above the fold. "The pattern of a sharp decrease in attention following the fold remains the same in 2018 as in 2010." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/); original 2010 study: [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention-original-research/)
- NN/g notes that the 2018 figure ignores page length. Short pages, or people giving up after two screens, could explain part of it. A third-party critique adds that the 57% figure measures share of attention, not scroll depth — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/); [Bigdelta (secondary)](https://bigdelta.com/blog/scroll-depth-benchmarks)
- NN/g, "The Fold Manifesto: Why the Page Fold Still Matters". "Users do scroll, but only if what's above the fold is promising enough." Scrolling is an extra action that has to be motivated. "Designs shouldn't need an arrow to tell users to scroll." Visual elements and compelling content near the top should draw the eye down. On small screens people scroll more, because they expect valuable information below — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto/)
- NN/g's information-foraging framing: people scroll only if they have reasons to — [NN/g video](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/scrolling-information-foraging/)
- NN/g, "Horizontal Attention Leans Left" (October 2017). With 120+ participants and 130,000+ fixations, 80% of fixations on general websites fell on the left half of the screen, versus 69% in the 2010 study. No newer update was found — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/horizontal-attention-leans-left/); [2010 original](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/horizontal-attention-original-research/)
- NN/g, "Homepage Design: 5 Fundamental Principles". Communicate who you are and what you do. Replace generic "Welcome" heroes with a meaningful tagline. The hero should carry a descriptive tagline and the unique value proposition. Purely decorative graphics get skipped. NN/g's bad example is Qurate, whose "Welcome" hero pushed the company description below the fold — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)
- Nielsen's older "113 Design Guidelines for Homepage Usability" says the most critical elements belong above the fold. It was written for 1024×768 screens, so its pixel specifics are dated — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/113-design-guidelines-homepage-usability/)

### Inferences
- What must be visible in the first viewport, on both mobile and desktop:
  - a plain-language statement of what the studio does and for whom (for example, website redesigns for renovation firms, B&Bs and wedding venues)
  - one concrete reason to believe it (a before/after or a real client result)
  - one primary next step
  - a visible hint of the content below
- The 10-second rule means the headline has to make sense without reading the body copy. Cleverness that needs a second read costs some of those 10 seconds.
- Attention leans left (80% of fixations). The value proposition and CTA should sit in the left or center column on desktop, not in a right-side panel next to a large illustration.
- The 2018 data is desktop only. On phones the first screen holds even less, so priorities are stricter.

### Gaps
- No NN/g mobile-specific eyetracking update on the fold (2019–2026) was found. The latest quantitative fold data is from 2018 and was gathered on desktop.
- The original Liu et al. (Microsoft Research) paper was not retrieved, so the Weibull parameters and the share of pages with negative aging were not verified.

## 3. Eye-tracking scanning patterns: F-pattern, layer-cake, spotted, commitment, Z-pattern

### Takeaway
The F-pattern is a failure mode. It appears when text has little formatting, such as walls of text with no headings, bolding or bullets. The layer-cake pattern, where readers fixate on headings and subheadings and dip into the body selectively, is the most effective scanning behavior, and good headings encourage it. The Z-pattern and Gutenberg diagram are widely repeated design heuristics with little controlled eyetracking evidence for web pages.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g, "F-Shaped Pattern of Reading on the Web: Misunderstood, But Still Relevant" (2017 update of the 2006 study). The F-pattern is the default when a page or section has little or no formatting for the web — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)
- NN/g, "Text Scanning Patterns: Eyetracking Evidence" (Pernice, 2017, per secondary citation) lists four main patterns. Ordered roughly from least to most effective, they are F-pattern, spotted (skipping large chunks to hunt for a specific item such as a link, number or keyword), layer-cake and commitment (fixating on nearly everything, typically when highly motivated) — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/); [Codecademy summary](https://www.codecademy.com/article/how-users-scan)
- NN/g, "The Layer-Cake Pattern of Scanning Content on the Web" (2019). It is defined as "fixations made mostly on the page's headings and subheadings, with deliberate occasional fixations on the (body) text in between." Most of the time it ensures users find what they're looking for, if it is on the page — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)
- NN/g says scanning patterns adapt to the current task ("Scanning Patterns on the Web Are Optimized for the Current Task") — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/eyetracking-tasks-efficient-scanning/)
- On the Z-pattern and Gutenberg diagram, design writers note there is "little empirical evidence" for claims that Gutenberg/Z layouts improve reading rhythm or comprehension. The Gutenberg diagram describes eye movement over evenly distributed, homogeneous information — [Vanseo Design](https://vanseodesign.com/web-design/3-design-layouts/); [Yoast](https://yoast.com/demystifying-viewing-patterns/)
- NN/g, "Photos as Web Content" (2010 eyetracking). Big feel-good decorative images are often completely ignored. Photos of real people and products are scrutinized. On one "About us" page, users spent 10% more time on real staff portraits than on the bios, even though the bios took up 316% more space. Users ignore stock photos of generic people. These numbers come from a single test page — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)

### Inferences
- To get layer-cake scanning instead of F-pattern skimming, every section needs a scannable, meaningful heading that carries the message on its own. Headings like "Il nostro metodo" are weaker than "In 3 settimane il tuo sito nuovo, senza che tu scriva una riga." Bold key phrases and short bullets help.
- The Z-pattern can still guide a sparse hero layout (logo top-left, nav top-right, headline, then CTA), but it should not be presented as research-backed.
- Real photos of the studio's actual client work and real people (the founders, real local businesses) are content that gets looked at. Generic stock, decorative illustrations and abstract shapes are likely to be skipped.

### Gaps
- No controlled eyetracking study of Z-pattern hero layouts was found.
- No NN/g eyetracking data on mobile scanning patterns was retrieved.

## 4. Hero section best practice: value proposition, headline, one primary CTA, focal point, avoiding false bottoms

### Takeaway
NN/g's evidence-based guidance is consistent. The hero must say what you do and for whom: a descriptive tagline, not "Welcome." It must not be purely decorative, and it must not fill the viewport so completely that the page looks finished. That is the "illusion of completeness" or "false floor." Content should visibly peek above the fold. A full-screen hero with only a scroll arrow is an anti-pattern. Most "one primary CTA" advice is practitioner consensus rather than controlled research.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g, "The Illusion of Completeness: What It Is and How to Avoid It". Large hero graphics or videos push real content below the fold and give no cue to scroll, which NN/g calls a "false floor." False floors "are increasingly common with modern minimalist designs." Full-width horizontal lines and section breaks can create a visual barrier that reads as the end of the page (example: Virgin America). Generous white space can cause the same effect. The remedy is to let additional content peek above the fold — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/illusion-of-completeness/); [NN/g video](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/illusion-completeness/)
- NN/g, "Fold Manifesto". An opening full-page image with only an arrow as a scroll cue is criticized: "Designs shouldn't need an arrow to tell users to scroll." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto/)
- CXL's "false bottom" article reaches the same conclusions and credits NN/g for the horizontal-line example. The page could not be fetched for detail — [CXL](https://cxl.com/blog/false-bottom/)
- NN/g, carousels: some people see only the first frame of a rotating hero, or none at all, which argues for a single strong hero message — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)
- NN/g, homepage principles: the hero should hold the descriptive tagline and value proposition. Decorative or unhelpful graphics get skipped — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)
- Practitioner guidance (not research) suggests a fold-height hero at about 80–90% of viewport height, so the next section peeks in — [Developers Journey, Substack](https://developersjourney.substack.com/p/above-the-fold-in-web-design-and-development). Another agency source suggests a shorter hero brings later sections into view and encourages scrolling, while a full-screen hero focuses one message — [Evoluted](https://www.evoluted.net/blog/design/landing-page-design-high-converting-heroes)
- NN/g, "Scrolljacking 101". In NN/g's usability study, scrolljacking (changing scroll rate or direction against the OS setting) caused "significant threats to user control and freedom, discoverability, attention, efficiency, and task success." One participant scrolled, nothing happened, and they were "surprised and disoriented." NN/g separates this from scroll fading, which only triggers animations and is acceptable if persistent, responsive and used sparingly — [NN/g Scrolljacking 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/); [NN/g Scroll Fading 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scroll-fading-101/)
- Accessibility advocates add that scroll hijacking breaks spacebar and keyboard navigation and that parallax or depth effects can cause motion sickness — [Bruta11y](https://www.bruta11y.com/no-scrolljacking-please); [Greenlit Content](https://greenlitcontent.com/marketing/scrolljacking-the-good-the-bad-and-the-ugly)

### Inferences
- These findings apply directly to the studio site. Its task history includes "full-screen scenes with snap and progress nav." Mandatory scroll-snap and 100vh scenes together combine two documented risks: (a) every scene can read as a false floor, and (b) snap behavior can feel like scrolljacking. If snap is kept, it should be proximity snap or none, and each scene should be shorter than the viewport so the next one peeks in.
- A focal point and a single primary CTA ("Richiedi l'analisi gratuita del tuo sito") reduce the choices a first-time visitor faces within the 10-second window. Secondary actions such as "Guarda i lavori" should look visibly secondary. This is a design inference consistent with NN/g's emphasis on a "distinct starting point," not a measured effect.
- The best hero focal point for this audience is probably a real before/after of a local business site (renovation firm, B&B, wedding venue). It works as content (NN/g photos finding), as a credibility signal (Fogg) and as a prototypical "this is what you'd get."

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found showing that one CTA beats multiple CTAs in a homepage hero. The claim is widespread in CRO blogs but those sources are opinion.
- The CXL false-bottom article's data could not be fetched.
- No research was found on the 2025 scrolljacking usability study (Springer). Its results were not visible — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-16454-4_6)

## 5. Visual hierarchy, whitespace vs dead space, density and rhythm, and when empty space hurts

### Takeaway
Visual hierarchy uses scale, color, contrast and grouping to signal importance. Whitespace mainly works as a grouping device, with tight spacing inside a group and wider spacing between groups. There is good evidence that lower visual complexity improves appeal and findability (Tuch 2012; Baughan 2020). NN/g also documents one specific way large empty areas hurt: big whitespace bands and full-width breaks create false floors that stop scrolling. No research quantifying an "optimal" whitespace ratio was found.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g, "Visual Hierarchy in UX: Definition". Hierarchy directs attention through variations in color, contrast, scale and grouping. In the Spotify example, wider gaps separate groups and tighter gaps bind headings to their content — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/)
- Generous white space can create an illusion of completeness that discourages scrolling — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/illusion-of-completeness/)
- Higher visual complexity reduces search efficiency and information recall (165 participants) — [ACM DL, Baughan et al. 2020](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3313831.3376849?cid=99659527940)
- Low complexity plus high prototypicality gives the most appealing first impression — [Google Research, Tuch et al. 2012](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/)
- The "20–30% whitespace" rule of thumb that circulates in design blogs has no cited research behind it — [DocHipo (blog)](https://www.dochipo.com/white-space-in-graphic-design/)
- An agency article notes that "dead space" is a client-side worry about emptiness, while designers treat whitespace as a comprehension tool. This is opinion — [Oneupweb](https://www.oneupweb.com/blog/whitespace-or-dead-space-beware-the-horror-of-web-design/)
- On section length and progressive disclosure, NN/g's 2018 finding that 74% of viewing time falls in the first two screenfuls implies each additional screen must re-earn attention. Its foraging framing says people scroll only for an expected payoff — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/); [NN/g video](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/scrolling-information-foraging/)

### Inferences
- A working rule from the evidence: whitespace is good when it groups and separates. It becomes "dead space" when (a) it fills most of a viewport with no content cue below, which creates a false floor, (b) it separates a heading from the content it labels, which breaks grouping, or (c) it makes a section feel finished when it is not.
- Rhythm: alternating dense and airy sections is fine, as long as every viewport-height slice of the page shows at least one meaningful heading, image or element. A full screen of near-empty background is the documented failure.
- Section length: front-load each section with its takeaway heading (layer-cake) so visitors who skim headings still get the argument. Put detail behind a "scopri di più" link or in an expandable element, rather than in long blocks.

### Gaps
- No primary study was found measuring engagement or scroll depth as a function of whitespace amount or section height.
- The full NN/g articles on whitespace and information density could not be retrieved, and no NN/g article dedicated to "too much whitespace" surfaced in search.

## 6. Aesthetic-usability effect and credibility judgments from visual design (Stanford / Fogg)

### Takeaway
Attractive interfaces are perceived as easier to use, and users forgive minor problems in them, though attractiveness cannot rescue severe usability failures. When people judge a site's credibility, the "design look" is the most often mentioned factor, by a wide margin. Stanford's guidelines tell sites to look professional or appropriate to their purpose and to show that a real organization stands behind them.

### Cited Findings
- Kurosu and Kashimura (Hitachi, CHI '95) tested 26 ATM UI variants with 252 participants. Aesthetic ratings correlated more strongly with perceived ease of use than with actual ease of use — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic%E2%80%93usability_effect)
- NN/g, "The Aesthetic-Usability Effect" (Moran, January 29, 2017). Users tolerate minor usability issues in attractive designs, which can mask problems in testing. Aesthetics cannot offset severe usability failures — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/)
- Fogg et al., "How do users evaluate the credibility of Web sites?" (DUX 2003). 2,684 participants evaluated live sites. "Design look" appeared in 46.1% of comments, the most frequent category, followed by information design/structure and information focus. Note that 46.1% is a share of comments, not of people, and some sources misquote it as "46% of consumers" — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/997078.997097); [ResearchGate](https://researchgate.net/publication/234826424_How_do_users_evaluate_the_credibility_of_Web_sites); [Stanford PDF](http://credibility.stanford.edu/pdf/How_Do_People_Evaluate_a_Web_Site's_Credibility_v37.pdf)
- Stanford Guidelines for Web Credibility (Fogg, May 2002) were based on three years of research with over 4,500 people. The ten guidelines are:
  1. Make it easy to verify information.
  2. "Show that there's a real organization behind your site."
  3. Highlight expertise.
  4. Show trustworthy people.
  5. Make contact easy.
  6. "Design your site so it looks professional (or is appropriate for your purpose)."
  7. Make it easy to use.
  8. Update often.
  9. Use restraint with promotional content.
  10. Avoid errors, including typos and broken links.

  Sources: [Stanford guidelines](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Web_Credibility_Project); [Simon Willison 2002 reproduction](https://simonwillison.net/2002/Jul/27/stanfordGuidelines/)
- Lindgaard et al. 2011: trustworthiness judgments made at 50 ms are largely driven by visual appeal — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1959022.1959023)
- Vendor claims such as "46% of web sales are lost on sites lacking trust elements," attributed to Stanford, are unsupported by the study — noted in search review of [Fogg et al.](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/997078.997097)

### Inferences
- For a design studio, the aesthetic-usability effect cuts both ways. A beautiful site earns forgiveness, but a prospect who hits a layout bug, cut-off text, janky mobile scroll or a confusing scroll-snap reads it directly as the quality of work they would get. NN/g's caveat that aesthetics cannot offset severe failures applies most strongly here.
- Fogg guidelines 2 and 4 suggest showing real founders' faces, a real address or territory, a P.IVA, real client names and real phone or WhatsApp contact. These also count as "photos as content" that get looked at.
