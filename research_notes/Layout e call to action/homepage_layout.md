# Homepage and landing-page layout patterns that convert for service businesses (one-person web studio, Milan)

Research note scope: section order and page length, hero composition, grid/width/rhythm, navigation, mobile ordering, bento/editorial/oversized type. Researched 2026-10-12. Tooling note: nngroup.com, crazyegg.com could not be fetched directly from this environment (DNS/proxy blocks), so NN/g findings below come from search-result extracts of the NN/g pages (titles and URLs are NN/g originals). Where a figure was only available second-hand it is flagged.

Evidence labels used below: **[DATA]** = measured study/benchmark; **[TEST]** = vendor/agency A/B test (single site, not independent); **[GUIDELINE]** = usability-research-firm guidance based on qualitative studies; **[OPINION]** = practitioner opinion/framework.

---

## 1. Section order and page length (hero > proof/demo > problem > offer > process > pricing > FAQ > final CTA; long vs short for considered purchases)

### Takeaway
No source tested found a universal "winning" section order; the defensible rule is that length must match visitor awareness and decision cost (Copyhackers/Wiebe, CXL), while attention data (NN/g) says most viewing time is spent in the first two screens, so the essential argument (what, for whom, proof, CTA) must be complete there and every later section must earn its place. For a no-client studio selling a considered purchase (a website redesign costs the buyer real money), a medium-long page with a short, front-loaded top and a repeatable CTA is the best-supported choice.

### Cited Findings
- [DATA] In NN/g's 2018 eyetracking analysis (130,000+ fixations, 1920x1080 screens), users spent about 57% of page-viewing time above the fold and 74% in the first two screenfuls (up to 2160px). In 2010 the above-fold share was about 80%. The sharp drop after the fold persisted across both studies. — [NN/g, Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/) (figures as extracted via search; also summarized by [Univ. of St Andrews digital comms blog](https://digitalcommunications.wp.st-andrews.ac.uk/2020/09/21/scrolling-and-the-illusion-of-completeness/))
- [DATA caveat] NN/g notes the analysis disregarded maximum page length, so the fall-off may reflect short pages or users giving up after two screenfuls; a 2026 commentary adds it measured viewing time, not comprehension or conversion. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/); [pravinkumar.co commentary](https://www.pravinkumar.co/blog/how-far-down-a-long-page-attention-actually-goes-2026)
- [GUIDELINE] "What is visible on the page without requiring any action is what encourages us to scroll." — [NN/g, The Fold Manifesto](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto/)
- [DATA] Unbounce 2024 Conversion Benchmark Report: 41,000+ landing pages, 464M visitors, 57M conversions (Jul 2023 to Jul 2024). Copy at a 5th to 7th grade reading level converts best (reported 11.1%, about 56% better than professional-level copy; secondary coverage says "nearly twice"), and difficult words are the factor most strongly linked to lower conversion (correlation 62% stronger than in 2020). — [Unbounce CBR methodology](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/methodology/); [DMN summary](https://dmn.ca/unbounces-2024-conversion-benchmark-report-proves-that-attention-spans-are-declining-and-so-are-conversion-rates/) (the 11.1%/56% figures are from secondary summaries; verify against the report)
- [DATA] Unbounce word-count findings by vertical: lead-gen pages with 800+ words had a 33% lower median conversion rate than pages under 200 words (4Q16 data); education pages: 500 words or fewer recommended; ecommerce: under 300 words converts best; family support: under 200 words, but benefit "maybe an extra percent or two". — [MarketingProfs on Unbounce lead-gen benchmarks](https://www.marketingprofs.com/charts/2017/31924/lead-gen-landing-page-benchmarks-conversion-rates-and-content-metrics); [Unbounce industry pages](https://unbounce.com/industry/family-support-2/)
- [TEST] Copyhackers (Joanna Wiebe) tests conflict by context: for Crazy Egg a short Version D pulled 13% better than a long sales-page control; in another test a longer page gave +37.5% net signups; for Highrise a shorter page increased paid signups by 102.5%. — [Copyhackers, long-form sales page](https://copyhackers.com/long-form-sales-page/); [Crazy Egg, "It's not long or short"](https://crazyegg.com/blog/high-performing-landing-pages)
- [OPINION] Wiebe: top-of-funnel (less aware) prospects need long-form pages that walk them through each awareness stage; most-aware visitors need little. Stripe Atlas's guide, crediting Wiebe, uses the five-stage awareness model. — [Copyhackers, How to write a sales page](https://copyhackers.com/how-to-write-a-sales-page/); [Stripe Atlas, landing page copy](https://stripe.com/fr-be/guides/atlas/landing-page-copy)
- [OPINION/DATA mix] CXL cites Brown University research that people are most likely to buy when they feel they understand the product best, and that intuitive thinkers preferred short pages; recommends segment testing. CXL also reports 37signals found a short page raised conversions but expanding it made it worse. Marketing Experiments reported long copy beating short in three head-to-head tests (PPC, health product). — [CXL, Long form or short form](https://conversionxl.com/long-form-or-short-form/); [SEO SiteCheckup summary](https://seositecheckup.com/articles/the-long-and-short-of-landing-page-copy)
- [OPINION] Apexure: short pages suit visitors already aware of the offer; longer pages suit top-of-funnel visitors. — [Apexure, landing page length](https://www.apexure.com/blog/landing-page-length)
- [OPINION] Peep Laja (CXL, Speero, Wynter): homepage copy, not design, is often the real conversion bottleneck; messaging must reflect strategy rather than be patched. No published Laja/Wynter section-order template was found. — [Exit Five podcast with Peep Laja](https://media.exitfive.com/podcast/strategy-b2b-positioning-understanding-how-people-buy-and-building-wynter-peep-laja); [RevGenius RevCon recap](https://www.revgenius.com/mag/how-to-stand-out-in-saas-with-powerful-b2b-messaging-insights-from-peep-lajas-revcon-session)

### Inferences
- The studio's buyers (renovation firms, window fitters, B&B and agriturismo owners, wedding venues) are mostly problem-aware ("my site is old / I get no requests") but not solution-aware about what a redesign delivers. Per Wiebe/CXL that argues for a medium-long homepage (roughly 7 to 9 sections), not a one-screen page. The Unbounce word-count data concerns paid-traffic landing pages and argues for tight, plain copy within each section, not fewer sections.
- Because 74% of viewing time falls in the first ~2160px (desktop), the first two screens should already contain: what you do + for whom (headline), outcome (subhead), primary CTA, the makeover demo (the main proof, since there are no clients), and a risk-reducer (price-from or guarantee).
- Recommended order for this studio (synthesis, not a tested template):
  1. Hero: headline + subhead + primary CTA + secondary CTA + 3D iPhone visual + one-line risk reducer ("Preventivo gratuito in 24h", "price from X").
  2. Makeover demo (before/after animation): the proof substitute; place directly under the hero so it peeks in.
  3. Problem / cost of an old site (short, 3 points, sector-specific).
  4. What you get (offer): deliverables as 3 to 6 cards.
  5. Sector examples / demo sites (renovation, B&B, wedding venue): concrete, by vertical.
  6. Process (3 to 4 steps, with timeline).
  7. Pricing (packages or "from" price) with CTA.
  8. About the person (photo, Milan, why trust a one-person studio): replaces missing testimonials.
  9. FAQ (6 to 8 objections: cost, time, who writes content, hosting, what if I don't like it).
  10. Final CTA band + contact options (form, WhatsApp, phone).
- Without client logos/testimonials, the demo and the founder's face/credentials must carry proof; guarantees and transparent pricing become more important as risk-reducers.

### Gaps
- No primary Unbounce/CXL/Baymard study was found that ranks section orders for service homepages; the order above is a synthesis.
- The widely-repeated claim "pages under 100 words convert 50% better than 500 words" could not be verified in any Unbounce source.
- The often-quoted Crazy Egg long-homepage result (+30% from Conversion Rate Experts) could not be verified because crazyegg.com was unreachable; not used.
- Marketing Examples (Harry Dry) has no fetched page giving a section order; only his above-the-fold framework is documented (see section 2).

## 2. Hero composition (split vs centered vs full-bleed; text/visual ratio; first-viewport contents; hero height and "false floor")

### Takeaway
The first viewport must say what you do and for whom in plain words, show a visual of the outcome, and offer a CTA; Harry Dry's five parts (title, subtitle, visual, social proof, CTA) are the most specific practitioner checklist. The hero must not look "complete": the next section should visibly peek in (NN/g illusion of completeness), which argues against 100vh full-screen heroes and scroll-snapped full-screen scenes.

### Cited Findings
- [OPINION] Harry Dry framework for above the fold: (1) explain the value you provide (title), (2) explain how you'll create it (subtitle), (3) let the user visualize it (visual), (4) make it believable (social proof), (5) make the next step easy (CTA). — [Not My Job newsletter summarizing Harry Dry](https://not-my-job.beehiiv.com/p/how-to-write-landing-pages-that-sell-above-the-fold) (secondary); [Marketing Examples](https://marketingexamples.com/)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g: homepages, especially of newer or less-known brands, need a short tagline that states what the company does; it is the place to signal differentiation "if you can do it succinctly"; vague slogans leave users unsure what is sold. — [NN/g, Top 10 Guidelines for Homepage Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-ten-guidelines-for-homepage-usability/); [NN/g, Tagline Blues](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tagline-blues-whats-the-site-about/)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g: value propositions should state the user outcome, not the mechanism ("Powered by AI" is not a value proposition). — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/powered-by-ai-is-not-a-value-proposition/)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g: "Get Started" style generic CTAs stop users; CTA labels should say what happens. — [NN/g, "Get Started" Stops Users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/get-started/) (title only confirmed via search)
- [GUIDELINE] Illusion of completeness (Tognazzini, 1998; NN/g): a first screenful tends to look complete and users assume they have seen everything unless the screen cuts through an element or a line of text. Hero banners and heavy white space are common culprits; the fix is "breaking" content (partially visible images/text) at the fold. It also applies to horizontal carousels. — [NN/g video, Illusion of Completeness](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/illusion-completeness/); [Liferay Design](https://liferay.design/articles/best-practices/illusion-of-completeness); [Webflow, Breaking the illusion of completeness](https://webflow.com/blog/breaking-the-illusion-of-completeness)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g recommends a static hero over a rotating carousel; auto-forwarding carousels are ignored/annoying; anything that looks like an ad triggers banner blindness. — [NN/g, Carousel Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/); [NN/g, Auto-Forwarding](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/auto-forwarding/); [NN/g, Banner Blindness Revisited](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/)
- [GUIDELINE] Text over images needs at least 4.5:1 contrast (3:1 for large text); use a scrim/overlay, blur, or text shadow. — [NN/g, Text over images](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-over-images/)
- [OPINION] Common hero patterns are split, centered and full-bleed image. — [Preline hero examples](https://preline.co/examples/hero-sections.html); [Omniconvert, hero examples 2026](https://www.omniconvert.com/blog/hero-section-examples/)

### Inferences
- For this studio: split hero on desktop (text left about 55%, iPhone visual right about 45%; equivalently a 7/5 split on a 12-column grid). Text left matches left-to-right scanning and keeps the headline readable; the iPhone is the "visualize it" element. A centered hero would push the phone below the copy and lengthen the first screen.
- Hero height: set to content height with a cap of roughly 85 to 90vh (or min(820px, 88vh)) so the top of the makeover section (a heading or the top edge of the "old site" frame) is visible on 1366x768, 1440x900 and 390x844 screens. Avoid 100vh plus scroll-snap scenes: each snapped scene is a designed "false floor".
- First viewport checklist: headline in plain Italian (what + for whom: e.g. local businesses), subhead with outcome, primary CTA ("Richiedi un'anteprima gratuita" style: says what happens), secondary text link ("Guarda la trasformazione" scrolling to the makeover), a proof line (no clients yet: a guarantee, "price from", turnaround time, or "Milano, una persona, risposta in 24h"), and the iPhone.
- Keep the before/after animation user-paced or clearly looping; do not use an auto-advancing carousel for demo sites.

### Gaps
- No source gave a tested optimal text/visual width ratio; the 55/45 figure is a design convention, not data.
- No tested data was found on split vs centered hero conversion for service sites.

## 3. Grid, max width, line length, section spacing and rhythm; how many sections is too many

### Takeaway
Hard numbers exist only for line length: about 50 to 75 characters (Baymard suggests capping text at ~70ch / 34em; WCAG 1.4.8 says 80 or fewer). Grid, container width and spacing values are conventions rather than research findings. "Too many sections" has no researched cut-off; the constraint is that each section must carry a distinct argument, and repeated CTAs help long pages.

### Cited Findings
- [GUIDELINE] Baymard: optimal line length roughly 50 to 75 characters; implement with font-relative max-widths of around 70ch or 34em, adjusted for the typeface; line length is only one component of readability. — [Baymard, Readability: The Optimal Line Length](https://baymard.com/blog/line-length-readability)
- [GUIDELINE] WCAG 1.4.8 (AAA): lines of 80 characters or fewer. Bringhurst: 45 to 75 characters, 66 ideal. Smashing Magazine (2009 survey): classic books say 55 to 75, practice skews 75 to 85. — [Smashing Magazine, Size Matters](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2014/09/balancing-line-length-font-size-responsive-web-design/); [Smashing Magazine, Typographic design patterns](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2009/08/typographic-design-survey-best-practices-from-the-best-blogs/)
- [GUIDELINE] On small screens an ideal measure conflicts with readable font size; design the mobile/tablet measure first. — [Smashing Magazine, Size Matters](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2014/09/balancing-line-length-font-size-responsive-web-design/)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g common region: containers (backgrounds, cards) create perceived groupings; this is the mechanism behind alternating section backgrounds. — [NN/g, Principle of Common Region](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/common-region/)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g recommends sticky or partially sticky menus on long pages so users do not have to scroll back to the top to act. — [NN/g, Menu-Design Checklist](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/menu-design/)

### Inferences
- Concrete spec (convention, consistent with the above): 12-column grid, container max-width 1200 to 1280px, 24px gutters desktop / 16px mobile side padding; body text max-width 65ch to 70ch (about 640 to 700px at 17 to 18px); headlines max about 18 to 22ch per line.
- Vertical rhythm: section padding about 96 to 128px desktop, 56 to 72px mobile; use an 8px spacing scale. Alternate background tones every 1 to 2 sections (e.g. white / pale tint / navy band for the final CTA) to mark argument boundaries via common region, rather than alternating every section mechanically.
- Section budget: 8 to 10 sections total for this homepage; beyond that, move detail (full pricing, full process, sector pages) to inner pages and link to them. Put a CTA after the makeover, after pricing, and at the end.

### Gaps
- No research-backed figure for container max width, section padding, or maximum section count was found; those numbers are design conventions.
- Unbounce/CXL data on alternating backgrounds or visual density vs conversion was not found.

## 4. Navigation: number of items, sticky/hiding headers, CTA in nav, mobile menu

### Takeaway
NN/g data favors visible (or combo) navigation over a hamburger, especially on desktop, and favors a partially persistent header (hides on scroll down, reappears on small scroll up) with an opaque background. For a small studio site with 4 to 5 destinations, show links visibly on desktop and keep a visible CTA button in the header on all breakpoints.

### Cited Findings
- [DATA] NN/g hidden vs visible navigation study (2017, 179 UK participants, 6 sites, desktop and mobile): hiding main navigation cut discoverability "almost in half", lengthened task time and raised perceived difficulty; effect larger on desktop than phones. Secondary summary gives users 39% slower on desktop and 15% slower on mobile with hidden nav, and a ~20% discoverability drop vs visible/combo (figures differ between summaries; verify). Best performers used combo navigation (some links visible, rest hidden) placed prominently at the top. — [NN/g, Hamburger Menus and Hidden Navigation Hurt UX Metrics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/); [NN/g, Methodology](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hidden-navigation-methodology/); [NN/g, Beyond the Hamburger (mobile)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/find-navigation-mobile-even-hamburger/); [Smart Insights summary](https://www.smartinsights.com/digital-marketing-strategy/are-hamburger-menus-hidden-navigation-good-for-ux/)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g: the hamburger icon is now recognizable, but visible navigation remains stronger because people forget to check hidden menus. — [NN/g, Hamburger-Menu Icon Today](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menu-icon-recognizability/)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g sticky headers: partially sticky header reappears as soon as the user scrolls up; trigger after a few pixels of upward scroll, with a 300 to 400ms slide-in; minimal animation (only for shrinking a large header or partially persistent headers); header must be opaque and contrast with content; avoid "stalker" headers that slide in after a delay. — [NN/g, Sticky Headers: 5 Ways to Make Them Better](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g recommends sticky/partially sticky menus on long pages. — [NN/g, Menu-Design Checklist](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/menu-design/); [PDF checklist](https://media.nngroup.com/media/articles/attachments/PDF_Menu-Design-Checklist.pdf)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g mobile patterns primer covers navigation bars/tab bars for few top-level items vs hamburger for many. — [NN/g, Basic Patterns for Mobile Navigation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-navigation-patterns/) (title only; content not extracted)

### Inferences
- Desktop header: logo left, 4 to 5 text links (e.g. Come funziona, Esempi, Prezzi, Chi sono, FAQ), one filled orange CTA button right ("Preventivo gratuito"). Height about 64 to 72px; opaque navy or white.
- Behavior: partially persistent (hide on down-scroll, reveal on up-scroll after ~8 to 10px, 300ms ease). Keep the CTA button reachable when revealed.
- Mobile header: logo + visible CTA button + menu button labeled "Menu" (combo nav); never hide the CTA inside the hamburger.

### Gaps
- Exact NN/g header-height limit (as % of viewport) could not be extracted; not reported.
- No primary data found on the conversion effect of a CTA button in the nav specifically.

## 5. Mobile layout order: hero visual vs text stacking; sticky bottom bars

### Takeaway
Research does not give a tested rule for mobile stacking; the attention data (users judge from the first screen) and the illusion-of-completeness guidance support putting headline + CTA first and a cropped visual peeking below. Sticky bottom CTA bars show positive but highly variable lifts in vendor (ecommerce) tests.

### Cited Findings
- [DATA/GUIDELINE] First-screen attention concentration and the illusion of completeness (see sections 1 and 2) apply to mobile as well, including horizontal swipe carousels. — [NN/g, Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/); [Liferay Design](https://liferay.design/articles/best-practices/illusion-of-completeness)
- [TEST] Sticky mobile CTA results (ecommerce, vendor-published): +69.25% conversion and +42.8% cart completion for a sticky "Pump Finder" CTA (13 days, 97.4% significance); +3.7% conversion for a persistent bottom add-to-cart bar on mobile while desktop declined; a sticky ATC with size selectors gave +3.8% add-to-cart but 0.0% conversion lift and lower revenue. Bottom placement is easier one-handed. — [Blend Commerce tests](https://blendcommerce.com/blogs/ab-tests-shopify/improving-product-discovery-on-mobile); [Clean Commit, persistent bottom bar](https://cleancommit.io/ab-tests/transforming-the-sticky-add-to-cart-into-a-persistent-bottom-bar/); [Blend Commerce, sticky ATC](https://blendcommerce.com/blogs/ab-tests-shopify/better-placed-sticky-add-to-cart-product-page-conversion)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g: hidden navigation is slower and less discoverable on mobile too (15% slower in one summary). — [NN/g, Beyond the Hamburger](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/find-navigation-mobile-even-hamburger/)

### Inferences
- Mobile hero stack: (1) headline, (2) subhead (max 2 to 3 lines), (3) primary CTA full-width (min 48px tall), (4) proof line, (5) iPhone visual cropped/scaled so its top shows within the first screen (about 390x844 viewport) and it bleeds past the fold to invite scrolling. Do not place the 3D phone first: on a 390px screen it would consume the whole first viewport and push the value proposition below the fold.
- Sticky bottom bar on mobile only, appearing after the hero CTA scrolls out of view, hidden near the final CTA/footer and when the keyboard is open; two actions (WhatsApp/Chiama + Preventivo) fit the local-business audience, who often prefer phone/WhatsApp. Respect `env(safe-area-inset-bottom)`. Treat the lift as a hypothesis: the evidence is ecommerce-only.
- Makeover animation on mobile: show before/after stacked or as a slider within the viewport width; avoid horizontal carousels with no visible next card.

### Gaps
- No NN/g or Baymard primary study found on hero image-vs-text stacking order on mobile.
- No sticky-bar test data for service/lead-gen sites was found (only ecommerce).

## 6. Bento grids, editorial layouts, oversized type: when they help vs hurt

### Takeaway
No usability research (NN/g, Baymard) on bento grids or oversized display type was found; available sources are design blogs. The consensus opinion: bento helps when tile size encodes importance and reading order is clear; it hurts when used decoratively or when hierarchy is visual only.

### Cited Findings
- [OPINION] Bento tile size should follow content importance, not decoration; "hierarchy, not novelty" makes bento work; tiles need a clear order for screen readers and adequate contrast. — [vp0, bento box UI](https://vp0.com/blogs/bento-box-ui-mobile-app-inspiration)
- [OPINION] Bento grids "are powerful, but they're not a universal solution." — [SaaSFrame, Designing Bento Grids That Actually Work (2026)](https://www.saasframe.io/blog/designing-bento-grids-that-actually-work-a-2026-practical-guide)
- [OPINION] Visual hierarchy carried only by tile size is invisible to non-sighted users when the grid is just sibling divs; use semantic headings/lists. — [21st.dev, React bento grid components](https://21st.dev/blog/react-bento-grid-components)
- [DATA] Unbounce: plain, low-reading-level copy converts best and difficult words hurt (relevant to oversized headline wording: short plain words). — [Unbounce CBR methodology](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/methodology/)
- [GUIDELINE] NN/g common region: containers create groupings, which is the perceptual basis of bento cards. — [NN/g, Common Region](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/common-region/)

### Inferences
- Use bento for the "What you get" offer section (one large tile for the main promise, e.g. "Sito nuovo in 2 settimane", smaller tiles for deliverables: mobile, Google Maps/SEO locale, WhatsApp button, booking/request form, hosting). On mobile collapse to a single column in priority order.
- Oversized type: fine for the hero headline (e.g. clamp(40px, 6vw, 80px) desktop, about 36 to 44px mobile) if it is 6 to 10 plain words; avoid giant type that forces the CTA or visual below the first viewport on 1366x768 laptops.
- Editorial layouts (asymmetric, magazine-style) suit the "About the person" and sector story sections; keep the conversion path sections (hero, pricing, FAQ, CTA) on a predictable grid.

### Gaps
- No NN/g/Baymard/CXL research specifically on bento grids or oversized typography found; all guidance here is practitioner opinion.
- Lapa Ninja / Land-book collections were not surveyed in this pass (tool-call budget).
